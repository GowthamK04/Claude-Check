## 1. The spine — three stages of colonialism

**The one line:** *the stage decides the policy* — Britain's purpose changed three times, and policy followed. (Bipan Chandra; R. P. Dutt, *India Today*, 1940)

| Stage | Wanted | Means | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I. Mercantile 1757–1813** | Indian goods and revenue, without paying in British exports | Monopoly; **Diwani 1765**; "investment" = goods bought with Bengal revenue; weavers coerced by advances (*dadni*) | Bengal famine 1770, revenue still collected |
| **II. Free trade 1813–1858** | Market for Lancashire, raw materials | **Charter Act 1813** ends monopoly (except tea, China); one-way free trade; Dalhousie's railways, telegraph, post | India: cloth exporter → cloth importer |
| **III. Finance imperialism 1858–1947** | Field for British capital; steady payments | Railways with guaranteed interest; plantations, mines, banks; Home Charges | India's export surplus settles Britain's deficits |

> **Answer line:** "British economic policy in India was not one policy but three — plunder under the Company, free trade for Lancashire after 1813, and an outlet for British capital after 1858."

## 2. Part A — British policies

### 1857 as a watershed (2016) — *changed allies, not aims*

| Domain | Before 1857 | After 1857 |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | Company | **Crown**: GoI Act 1858, Secretary of State, Viceroy |
| Princes | Annexation: Satara 1848, Jhansi 1853, Nagpur 1854, **Awadh 1856** | No annexation (Proclamation 1858); **adoption sanads 1862**; princes as "breakwaters" (Canning) |
| Landed class | Awadh taluqdars dispossessed (1856 summary settlement) | Taluqdars **restored**; zamindars as loyal pillars |
| Social reform | Sati 1829, widow remarriage 1856 | **Non-interference**; exception Age of Consent 1891 |
| Army | Sepoys far outnumber Europeans | **Peel Commission 1859**: more Europeans, artillery European, mixed units, "martial races" |
| Communities | — | **Divide and rule**: Muslims blamed, then courted (Hunter 1871); separate electorates 1909 |
| Finance | Company finance | **Income tax 1860 (James Wilson)**; finance imperialism |

> **Answer line:** "1857 did not change what Britain wanted from India; it changed whom Britain relied on to get it."

### Administrative policies (Spectrum's eight)
1. **Divide and rule** — princes vs people, provinces, castes, Hindus vs Muslims; partition of Bengal 1905; separate electorates 1909; Communal Award 1932.
2. **Hostility to educated Indians** — Dufferin: Congress a "microscopic minority" (1888); Curzon: Congress "tottering to its fall" (1900).
3. **Alliance with zamindars** — tenancy protection late: Bengal Rent Act 1859, Bengal Tenancy Act 1885.
4. **Retreat from social reform** after 1858.
5. **Starved social services** — army the largest expenditure.
6. **Labour legislation** — partly Lancashire's pressure; plantations excluded.
7. **Press curbs** — Vernacular Press Act 1878 (Lytton), repealed 1882 (Ripon).
8. **White racism** — **Ilbert Bill 1883** (Ripon): Indian judges to try Europeans; diluted 1884 (jury half European).

### Labour Acts
| Year | Act | Content |
|---|---|---|

### Paramountcy — five phases (≈ two-fifths of the subcontinent under princes)
| Phase | Policy | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| 1740–1765 | Struggle for equality | Company under Mughal authority; Carnatic, Plassey, Buxar |
| 1765–1813 | **Ring fence** | Buffer states (Awadh); **subsidiary alliance 1798** (Wellesley) |
| 1813–1857 | **Subordinate isolation** | Lord Hastings; no foreign relations; Residents; lapse and annexation |
| 1857–1935 | **Subordinate union** | No annexation but may depose (Baroda 1875); Royal Titles Act 1876; Curzon's paternalism; **Chamber of Princes 1921**; **Butler Committee 1927** — "paramountcy must remain paramount" |
| 1935–1947 | **Equal federation** | 1935 federation optional, never formed; princes used against nationalists; paramountcy lapses 1947 |

### Frontier policy (paid for by Indian revenue — so part of the drain)
- **Nepal:** war 1814–16 → **Treaty of Sagauli 1816** (Kumaon, Garhwal ceded; Gurkha recruitment).
- **Burma:** 1824–26 (Amherst) → **Yandabo 1826** (Burma gives up Assam, Manipur; cedes Arakan, Tenasserim); 1852 (Dalhousie, Pegu); 1885 (Dufferin) → Upper Burma annexed 1886; separated 1937.
- **Afghanistan:** 1839–42 (Auckland, disaster); Lawrence's "masterly inactivity"; **Lytton's forward policy** → 1878–80, Gandamak 1879; **Durand Line 1893** (Lansdowne); 1919 war, Treaty of Rawalpindi.
- **Bhutan:** Duar War 1864–65 → **Sinchula 1865**.
- **Tibet:** **Younghusband 1903–04** (Curzon) → Treaty of Lhasa 1904; Anglo-Russian Convention 1907.

## 3. Land revenue — the engine

| | **Permanent (Zamindari)** | **Ryotwari** | **Mahalwari** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Area | **~19%** | **~51%** | **~30%** |
| Who, when | **Cornwallis 1793**, with John Shore | **Alexander Read** (Baramahal 1792), **Thomas Munro** (Madras 1820); Bombay: Elphinstone, survey by Wingate & Goldsmid (1830s) | **Holt Mackenzie** (Reg. VII, **1822**); **R. M. Bird & James Thomason** (Reg. IX, **1833**, Bentinck) |
| Where | Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, Varanasi division, northern Madras | Madras, Bombay; later Assam, Coorg | NWP, Punjab, parts of Central India |
| Who pays | Zamindar (owner) | Ryot directly | Village/mahal jointly via **lambardar** |
| Demand | Fixed for ever: **10/11 state, 1/11 zamindar** | ~half net produce; revised every 20–30 yrs | ⅔ of rental (1822) → ½ (**Saharanpur rules 1855**) |
| Enforcement | **Sunset Law** — sold if unpaid by sunset | Eviction, sale | Joint liability |
| Who lost | Old zamindars; ryots became tenants-at-will; state lost rising rent | Ryot: high demand, no remission, moneylender | Small holders into debt |

**Common consequences:** land becomes a **commodity** → **moneylender** gains (*sahukar, mahajan*) · **new landlordism**, absenteeism, **subinfeudation** · rigidity in bad years → distress sales · **litigation** · revolt: **Deccan Riots 1875** → **Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act 1879**; **Punjab Land Alienation Act 1900**.

> **Answer line:** "All three settlements turned land into saleable property and demanded fixed cash revenue regardless of the harvest; together these made the moneylender, not the cultivator, the real beneficiary."

## 4. Agriculture — impoverishment, stagnation, forced commercialisation

- **Impoverishment:** high revenue, rents, illegal cesses, evictions, debt, more landless labourers, fragmentation.
- **Stagnation:** **George Blyn (1966)** — foodgrain output grew **~0.1% a year, 1891–1947** (British India), below population growth → food per head fell. *(Foodgrains only.)*
- **Crops:** **indigo** (planter coercion; Indigo Revolt 1859–60; *Nil Darpan*; declined after **synthetic dye**) · **cotton** (American Civil War boom 1861–65, then crash → Deccan Riots) · **jute** · **tea** (Assam; Workmen's Breach of Contract Act 1859) · **opium** (state monopoly, China) · sugarcane, groundnut.
- **Why "forced":** driven by revenue in cash, debt, planters' contracts; profits to traders, exporters, moneylenders; food land diverted; world prices reach the village.
- **Counter-case:** rich-peasant strata in **Punjab canal colonies, Gujarat (Patidars), coastal Andhra** → **differentiation**, not uniform ruin. **Imran Ali, *The Punjab under Imperialism* (1988)**.

## 5. Deindustrialisation (2017, 2024)

**Definition:** a fall in the **share of the population living by industry** — not just a fall in output. India's version = **ruralisation**.

**Causes, layered for "how far":**
1. **Company coercion first** — late-18th-c Bengal: monopoly buying, advances, below-market prices (**predates** the Industrial Revolution's effect).
2. **Loss of patrons** — courts annexed; Dacca muslin, Lucknow, Murshidabad crafts lose buyers; new elite turns to Western goods.
3. **One-way free trade** — heavy duties on Indian cloth in Britain; British goods near duty-free after 1813; **import duties abolished 1882**.
4. **Industrial Revolution** — machine cloth cheaper on coarse and medium cloth.
5. **Railways** — Manchester cloth into the village, raw cotton to the port.
6. **Raw cotton export** raised weavers' costs; **cotton excise 1896** on Indian mill cloth.
7. **No protecting state** — USA, Germany, Japan used tariffs; India could not.

**Consequence chain (2017):** artisans back to land → overcrowding, fragmentation → loss of off-season craft income, more landless labour → debt, low purchasing power, decayed craft towns → rural economy crippled and famine-prone.

**Evidence:**
- **Dacca** population **~150,000 → 30,000–40,000 by 1840** (Charles Trevelyan's evidence).
- **Amiya Kumar Bagchi (1976)** — Gangetic Bihar, industrial population **18.6% (1809–13) → 8.5% (1901)**, from Buchanan-Hamilton's survey and the 1901 Census.
- **Paul Bairoch** — India's share of world manufacturing **~25% (1750) → ~2% (1900)**.
- India: cloth exporter → **importer** of British cloth by the 1820s–30s.

**The debate:**
- **Morris D. Morris (1963)** "Towards a Reinterpretation of Nineteenth-Century Indian Economic History" — evidence of decline thin.
- Replies: **Bipan Chandra, Toru Matsui, Tapan Raychaudhuri** (*IESHR* symposium, **1968**).
- **Marika Vicziany (1979)** — contests Bagchi's reading of the data.
- **Tirthankar Roy** — handloom **weaving survived** using mill yarn; collapse was in **hand spinning** and luxury crafts.
- **Clingingsmith & Williamson (2005)** — decline began with post-Mughal disorder; productivity gap.
- **Prasannan Parthasarathi (2011)** — Britain's own cotton industry grew behind tariffs against Indian cloth.

> **Answer line:** "The Industrial Revolution was the necessary condition of India's deindustrialisation; colonial rule was the sufficient one — it left India no tariff wall, no patrons and no modern industry to absorb the displaced."

## 6. Modern industry and railways

- **Cotton mill:** Bombay, **C. N. Davar, 1854**; later Ahmedabad. **Jute:** Rishra, **1855**. **Coal:** Raniganj. **TISCO 1907** (Jamshedpur). Plantations: tea, coffee.
- **Discriminating protection 1923** (after **Fiscal Commission 1921–22**) → steel, sugar, paper, cement.
- **Pattern:** foreign capital in jute, tea, coal, mining via **managing agencies**; Indian capital in cotton, later steel, sugar, cement; **no heavy/capital-goods industry**; clustered in **Bombay, Calcutta, Madras** → **regional imbalance** (2026 link).
- **Indian capitalists:** **FICCI 1927**; **Bombay Plan 1944** (Tata, Birla and others).
- **Railways:** **Dalhousie's Railway Minute 1853**; first passenger line **Bombay–Thane, 16 April 1853**; **guarantee system ~5% interest paid from Indian revenue**; built for troops, raw materials to ports, goods inland; rails and engines from Britain. **Counter:** national market; **Burgess & Donaldson (2010)** — railways reduced famine mortality from drought.

## 7. Drain of wealth

**Definition:** the unrequited transfer of part of India's national product to Britain, financed by India's export surplus.

**Four channels:**
1. **Home Charges** — interest on public debt in London, India Office costs, pensions and furloughs, army stores, **wars outside India**.
2. **Remittances** of British officials' salaries and savings.
3. **Interest and profits** on foreign capital (incl. guaranteed railway interest).
4. **"Invisibles"** — shipping, banking, insurance.
Before 1813: the **"investment"** — goods bought with Bengal's revenue.

**Theorists:**
- **Dadabhai Naoroji** — "England's Debt to India" (**1867**); *Poverty of India* (**1876**); *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (**1901**); per capita income **Rs 20 (1867–68)**; called the rule **"un-British"**.
- **R. C. Dutt** — *The Economic History of India*, **1902 and 1904**.
- **William Digby** — *'Prosperous' British India* (**1901**).
- Also M. G. Ranade, G. V. Joshi, **G. Subramania Iyer**, G. K. Gokhale.
- **Syed Ahmad Khan** did **not** accept it.
- **Counter:** **Theodore Morison**, *The Economic Transition in India* (**1911**) — payments bought administration, defence, capital. **Reply:** India chose neither the services nor their price; the debt came from wars and guarantees imposed on it.
- **Significance:** destroyed the "benevolent rule" idea; core of economic nationalism; fed Swadeshi.
- **Caution:** Utsa Patnaik's **~US$45 trillion (1765–1938)** is one contested estimate.

## 9. Famines (2022)

| Famine | Scale | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Bengal 1770** | perhaps **⅓** of Bengal's population; revenue maintained | — |
| **Orissa 1866** | ~1 million | **Campbell Commission** |
| **1876–78 Great Famine** | Madras, Bombay, Mysore, Hyderabad; **5.6–9.6 million**; grain exported; Lytton's Delhi Durbar 1877 | **Strachey Commission 1880 → Famine Code 1883**; Famine Insurance Fund |
| **1896–97 / 1899–1900** | almost every province; El Niño | **Lyall 1898** / **MacDonnell 1901** (Curzon); Irrigation Commission 1901–03 |
| **Bengal 1943** | **~3 million**; wartime | **Woodhead Commission 1944** → rationing, PDS |

**Digby's estimates of famine deaths:** ~1 m (1800–25) → ~0.4 m (1825–50) → ~5 m (1850–75) → **~15 m (1876–1900)**.

**Why the spurt — eight reasons:**
1. **Revenue before relief** (1770).
2. **Buffers lost** — craft income, grain stores, local relief obligations.
3. **Commercialisation and export** — grain exported in 1876–78.
4. **Market integration** — railways sent grain to the highest bidder (but also carried relief).
5. **Laissez-faire / Malthusian ideology** — Lytton; **Richard Temple's reduced ration (1877)**.
6. **No protective irrigation** in dry tracts till after 1880.
7. **El Niño droughts** 1876–78, 1896–1902 — **Mike Davis, *Late Victorian Holocausts* (2001)**.
8. **1943: war** — fall of Burma, denial policy, inflation — **Amartya Sen, *Poverty and Famines* (1981)**: **entitlement failure**, not food shortage.

**Counter-point:** no famine of the 1870s–90s scale between **1908 and 1942** (codes, railways, irrigation).

> **Answer line:** "Drought made the harvest fail; colonial economics made the failure fatal."

## 10. Indentured labour (2018)

- **Pull:** slavery abolished (Act **1833**, in force **1834**) → sugar plantations need labour.
- **Destinations in order:** **Mauritius 1834** → British Guiana 1838 → Trinidad 1845 → Natal 1860 → Suriname 1873 (Dutch) → **Fiji 1879** (last). **Over 1.3 million, 1834–1917.**
- **Push:** revenue pressure, debt, famine, deindustrialisation — **Bhojpuri belt** (Bihar, eastern UP), Madras.
- **Mechanism:** recruiters (**arkatis**), deception, depots at Calcutta and Madras, penal five-year contracts (*girmit* = "agreement").
- **Hugh Tinker, *A New System of Slavery* (1974).**
- **End:** **Gokhale's resolution 1910** → Natal recruitment ended **1911**; Gandhi, C. F. Andrews; recruitment stopped **1917**; formally ended **1920**.
- **Identity — yes, but reshaped:** kept faith (*Ramcharitmanas*, temples, Ramlila), song (**Geet-Gawai**, Mauritius, UNESCO intangible heritage), festival (Diwali a public holiday), memory (**Aapravasi Ghat**, World Heritage Site); lost caste (**jahaji bhai**), shifted language (Fiji Hindi, English, Creole); syncretic forms (chutney music); contested ethnic politics (Fiji, Guyana, Trinidad).

> **Answer line:** "The indentured diaspora preserved the core of its culture — faith, song and festival — while losing caste and much of its language; it survived by changing."

## 11. Verdict and historiography

| School | Names | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| Nationalist | Naoroji, Ranade, Dutt, Gokhale; **Bipan Chandra, *Rise and Growth of Economic Nationalism* (1966)** | Impoverishment by drain, deindustrialisation, over-taxation |
| Marxist | **R. P. Dutt, *India Today* (1940)**; Irfan Habib; Bagchi; Patnaik | Colonial structure serving British capital in three stages |
| Imperial / revisionist | Morison; Vera Anstey; **Morris**; **Tirthankar Roy** | Decline exaggerated; railways, law, markets |
| Global | **Bairoch**; Clingingsmith & Williamson; **Parthasarathi** | Part of the Great Divergence; policy and technology both |

**Verdict:** **growth in enclaves** (ports, railways, plantations, mills), **stagnation in the mass** (agriculture); per capita income nearly flat in the last half-century of rule (Maddison; Sivasubramonian). **Legacy:** poverty, food insecurity, thin industry in three port cities, regional imbalance → planning and land reform after 1947.

> **Answer line:** "Nationalists and revisionists disagree about how much and how fast, not about the direction of change."

## The traps

1. **Ryotwari ≠ Cornwallis.** Read and Munro. (Cancelled 2024 Prelims.)
2. **Indigo declined because of synthetic dye**, not the 1859–60 revolt.
3. **Factories Act 1881** — no wage fixing, no unions.
4. **Ilbert Bill** — jurisdiction over Europeans, not the press.
5. **Deindustrialisation** = falling **share of people in industry**, not just lower output.
6. **Modern industry existed** — say it was **small, late and concentrated**.
7. **Never present** Patnaik's $45 trillion or famine deaths as exact — write "about" or a range.
8. **"Bones of the cotton-weavers… bleaching the plains of India"** — write "a line quoted by Marx", not "Bentinck said".
9. **Blyn's 0.1%** is foodgrains only, not all agriculture.
10. **1857 changed allies, not aims** — don't write that British policy became benevolent.
