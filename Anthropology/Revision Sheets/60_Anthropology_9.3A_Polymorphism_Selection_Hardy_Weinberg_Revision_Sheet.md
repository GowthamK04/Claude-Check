## 1. Mendelian population and gene pool

- ==**Mendelian population** (Dobzhansky): an interbreeding community whose members share a common **gene pool** — all the alleles carried by all its members.==
- **India:** centuries of **endogamy** (marrying within the group) make many castes and tribes partly separate Mendelian populations. That is why some disorders are much commoner in particular communities.
- Two kinds of frequency:
  - **allele frequency** — the share of all copies of a gene that are one version (if 30 of 100 copies are "a", then q = 0.3);
  - **genotype frequency** — the share of people who are AA, Aa or aa.

## 2. Hardy–Weinberg — the still pond

🪝 *G. H. Hardy was proud that his mathematics had no practical use. His 1908 side-remark — Weinberg reached the same result that year — is now used every day in medicine and forensics.*

- **A common mistake, corrected:** dominant alleles do **not** become common by themselves. ==Mendelian inheritance alone leaves allele frequencies **unchanged**== — this steady state is **equilibrium**.
- ==**p + q = 1** · **p² + 2pq + q² = 1**==, where p² = AA, 2pq = Aa and q² = aa.
- ==**The five conditions:**==
  1. a very **large** population;
  2. **random** mating;
  3. no **mutation**;
  4. no **migration**;
  5. no **selection**.

**Worked example — albinism, 1 in 10,000 people affected**
1. q² = 0.0001, so q = 0.01.
2. p = 1 − 0.01 = 0.99.
3. ==Carriers = 2pq ≈ 0.02 = about **1 in 50** people.==
4. So there are roughly **200 carriers for every affected person**.

🪝 *This is why eugenic sterilisation could never get rid of a rare recessive disease: almost all copies of the allele hide in healthy carriers.*

**What the law is used for**
- **Estimating carriers** for screening programmes (sickle-cell, thalassaemia).
- **As a null model:** if real frequencies depart from it, some force is acting. ==An excess of homozygotes, for example, points to inbreeding or endogamy.==
- **Forensic DNA** probabilities.

> **Line:** ==Equilibrium is the default; every change in gene frequency points to a force== — mutation, migration, drift, non-random mating or selection.

## 3. Polymorphism and its types

🪝 *1950s, East Africa: **Allison** mapped the sickle-cell gene and found it matched the map of malaria. A gene that kills in double dose stayed common because its carriers survived malaria.*

- ==**Polymorphism** (Ford, 1940): two or more forms of a gene in one population, the rarest too common to be kept there by mutation alone — in practice, the rarer allele is at least **1%**.==
- **Human examples:** ABO, Rh and MN blood groups · HbS, HbE and the thalassaemias · G6PD · PTC tasting · lactase persistence · HLA.

| Type | What it is | Human example |
|---|---|---|
| **Balanced** | stable — selection holds both alleles | HbS with malaria; β-thalassaemia; G6PD; HLA |
| **Transient** | one allele in the process of replacing another | lactase persistence; HbS declining among African Americans |
| **Neutral** | no effect on fitness; frequency drifts by chance | much non-coding DNA |

**How balance is maintained**
- ==**Heterozygote advantage**== — the carrier is fitter than either homozygote.
- **Frequency-dependent selection** — a rare form is favoured because it is rare (rare HLA types resist new diseases).
- **Varying environments** — different alleles are favoured in different places or seasons.

==**Relaxed selection** (1996): take away malaria and carriers lose their advantage, so a balanced polymorphism becomes a transient one.==

## 4. Balanced polymorphism — the sickle-cell case

| Genotype | Blood | Malaria | Fitness |
|---|---|---|---|
| AA | normal | vulnerable | reduced |
| **AS** (carrier) | mostly normal | **resistant** | ==**highest**== |
| SS | sickle-cell anaemia | — | reduced |

**Result:** selection removes both homozygotes and keeps **both alleles**, giving a stable equilibrium.

🪝 *India's sickle-cell belt runs through tribal central and western India — Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Maharashtra, Gujarat — regions with a long history of malaria. In some groups a large share of people are carriers.*

**The same logic** applies to β-thalassaemia, HbE (in the north-east) and G6PD deficiency — all common where malaria was.

## 5. Natural selection

- **It needs three things:** variation + inheritance + differences in survival or reproduction.
- **It is measured by:** **fitness** (the relative number of offspring a genotype leaves) and the **selection coefficient** (how much less fit the weaker genotype is).

| Pattern | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Directional** | shifts the whole curve one way | lactase persistence; Tibetan **EPAS1** variant (thought to come from Denisovans) |
| **Stabilising** | removes both extremes | human birth weight |
| **Disruptive** | favours both extremes | rare in humans |
| **Balancing** | keeps two alleles | sickle-cell |

> ==Selection can **preserve** variation as well as remove it.== Polymorphism is often the footprint selection leaves behind.

## 6. The four names to know

| Name | Remember them for |
|---|---|
| **Hardy & Weinberg** (1908) | the equilibrium law, p² + 2pq + q² |
| **Dobzhansky** | the Mendelian population |
| **Ford** (1940) | the definition of polymorphism |
| **Allison** (1950s) | sickle-cell and malaria |

## The traps
1. Hardy–Weinberg does **not** make dominant alleles more common.
2. **q** (allele frequency) ≠ **q²** (frequency of affected people).
3. A carrier is the heterozygote, so carrier frequency is **2pq**.
4. **Balanced** (stable) ≠ **transient** (one allele replacing another).
5. Always pair the peppered moth with a **human** example.
