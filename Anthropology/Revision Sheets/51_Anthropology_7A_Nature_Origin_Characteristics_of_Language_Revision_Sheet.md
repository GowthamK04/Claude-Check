## 1. What language is

🪝 *A two-year-old understands "the purple elephant ate my homework" the first time she hears it — and could invent ten more like it.* That is **productivity**.

- **Sapir (1921), *Language*:** ==language is "a purely human and non-instinctive method of communicating ideas, emotions and desires by means of a system of voluntarily produced symbols."==
- **Working definition:** a culturally transmitted system of arbitrary symbols, organised by rules at several levels, through which a speech community communicates, thinks and passes on its culture.
- **Saussure, *Course in General Linguistics* (1916)**
  - The **sign** = **signifier** (sound-image) + **signified** (concept), and the link between them is **arbitrary**.
  - **Langue** is the shared system; **parole** is individual speech.
  - Meaning comes from **difference** within the system.
- **Chomsky, *Syntactic Structures* (1957) and *Aspects* (1965)**
  - **Competence** (tacit knowledge of the rules) vs **performance** (actual use).
  - An innate **language acquisition device (LAD)** and **universal grammar** explain how children learn fast from limited input — the "poverty of the stimulus".
- **Levels of language:** phonology → morphology → syntax → semantics → pragmatics.
- **Boas:** ==there are no "primitive" languages== — all are equally complex; the complexity is simply distributed differently.

## 2. Characteristics — Hockett's design features (1960; Hockett & Altmann 1968)

**List ten in any answer:**
1. Symbolic and arbitrary.
2. Rule-governed.
3. **Productive.**
4. **Displacement** — talk about what is absent.
5. **Duality of patterning** — meaningless sounds combine into meaningful words.
6. **Culturally transmitted**, though resting on a universal capacity.
7. Social.
8. Dynamic.
9. Universal.
10. Reflexive — language can talk about itself (and it allows prevarication, i.e. lying).

| Feature | Bee | Vervet | Signing ape | Human |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arbitrariness, semanticity | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Discreteness | no | yes | yes | yes |
| **Displacement** | partial | no | partial | yes |
| **Productivity** | partial | no | partial | yes |
| **Duality of patterning** | no | no | no | yes |
| **Cultural transmission** | no | partial | partial | yes |
| Prevarication, reflexiveness | no | partial | partial | yes |

- **Honeybee** (Karl von Frisch, Nobel 1973): the waggle dance points to nectar out of sight, but it is only ever about food or the nest.
- **Vervet monkeys** (Seyfarth, Cheney & Marler 1980): separate alarm calls for leopard, eagle and snake — but no way to say "yesterday".
- **Signing apes:** Washoe (the Gardners, from 1966), Koko, and Kanzi (lexigrams) learnt hundreds of signs.

🪝 *Washoe saw a swan and signed WATER BIRD. Her trainers called it a new word. Herbert Terrace, after years with the chimp Nim Chimpsky, said the apes were mostly copying their teachers.*

- **Terrace, "Can an Ape Create a Sentence?" (*Science*, 1979):** sentences did not grow longer, there was no syntax, and the apes rarely asked spontaneous questions.
- **Verdict:** apes and humans differ ==in degree for symbols, in kind for syntax==.

> **Answer line:** "No single design feature is unique to human language; the **combination** is."

## 3. Origin — the 2010 question

🪝 *Around 650 BCE, says Herodotus, Pharaoh Psammetichus raised two babies in silence. Their first word was "bekos" — Phrygian for bread — so he declared Phrygian the oldest language.* ==In **1866** the Société de Linguistique de Paris banned papers on the origin of language.==

### (a) Speculative theories — name them, then explain why they failed

| Theory | Claim | Associated with |
|---|---|---|
| **Bow-wow** | imitation of natural sounds | Herder; mocked by Max Müller 1861 |
| **Pooh-pooh** | instinctive cries of emotion | interjectional theory |
| **Ding-dong** | natural resonance between sound and thing | Müller (later abandoned) |
| **Yo-he-ho** | chants of collective labour | Ludwig Noiré |
| **La-la** | play, love, song | Otto Jespersen 1922 |
| **Ta-ta** | the mouth imitating hand gestures | Richard Paget 1930 |

**Why they failed:** none can be tested, each explains a few words but not grammar, and all assume a single "first word".

### (b) Biological evidence — the capacity

🪝 *The London "KE family": three generations struggling to sequence speech sounds, traced in 2001 to a one-letter change in **FOXP2**.* ==It is not "the language gene"== — it is a gene for speech motor sequencing.

- **Vocal tract:** the descended larynx enlarges the throat for varied vowels (Lieberman).
- **Hyoid bone:** a near-modern Neanderthal hyoid from **Kebara**, Israel (Arensburg et al. 1989).
- **Breath control:** the enlarged thoracic canal that fine breath control needs is absent in the Nariokotome *H. erectus* boy, present in Neanderthals (MacLarnon & Hewitt 1999).
- **Brain:** Broca's area (1861) and Wernicke's area (1874); a Broca's-area bulge on the endocast of *H. habilis* (Tobias; Falk).
- **Genes:** FOXP2 in the KE family (**Lai et al. 2001**); Neanderthals shared the human variant (**Krause et al. 2007**).
- **Gesture first:** mirror neurons (Rizzolatti & Arbib 1998); Corballis, *From Hand to Mouth* (2002).
- **Social bonding:** **Dunbar (1996)** — gossip as "vocal grooming" once groups grew to about 150.
- **Protolanguage:** **Bickerton (1990)** — words came before syntax.
- **Saltation:** Berwick & Chomsky, *Why Only Us* (2016) — a single "Merge" mutation.

### (c) Cultural evidence — the use

- **Labour:** Engels, "The Part Played by Labour in the Transition from Ape to Man" (1876).
- **Symbol:** Blombos Cave ochre (c. 75,000 years ago) and Upper Palaeolithic art; Deacon, *The Symbolic Species* (1997) — symbols and the brain co-evolved.
- **Nicaraguan Sign Language:** 🪝 *deaf children in Managua schools from the late 1970s built a language from scratch, and each younger cohort made it more grammatical.* A language needs a community, not just a brain.
- **Creoles:** children turn a pidgin into a full creole — they supply the grammar.
- **One origin or many** (monogenesis vs polygenesis): unresolved.

### (d) When?

- Estimates range from about 2 million years ago to 50,000 years ago.
- Middle view: ==a protolanguage with *H. erectus*; full syntax with *H. sapiens*, c. 200,000–100,000 years ago.==

> **Answer line:** "Biology explains the **capacity** for language; culture explains its **use** — and the two co-evolved."

## 4. Language, culture and thought — the core strand

🪝 *Benjamin Lee Whorf was a fire-prevention inspector. Workers smoked beside petrol drums labelled "empty" — empty of petrol, but full of explosive vapour. The word had made them careless.*

- **Sapir (1929):** people are very much at the mercy of the particular language of their society. *(Close paraphrase.)*
- **Whorf (1940):** we dissect nature along lines laid down by our native languages. *(Close paraphrase.)*
- **Strong version — linguistic determinism:** language fixes thought. **Weak version — linguistic relativity:** language influences habitual thought and attention.

| Position | Who |
|---|---|
| Language is part of culture; race, language and culture vary independently | **Boas, *Handbook of American Indian Languages* 1911** |
| Strong determinism | Whorf, as popularly read |
| Weak relativity | Sapir 1929; Levinson; Lucy |
| Culture shapes vocabulary | Evans-Pritchard (Nuer cattle terms); Conklin (Hanunóo plants) |
| Culture as grammar-like knowledge | Goodenough 1957 (6-I) |
| Universalism | Chomsky; **Berlin & Kay 1969**; Pinker 1994 |

**Evidence against the strong version**
- **Hopi time:** Malotki, *Hopi Time* (1983), documented abundant Hopi time expressions — Whorf's key example refuted.
- 🪝 **The "Eskimo snow words" are a myth.** Boas mentioned a few roots (1911); retellings inflated them. Martin 1986; ==Pullum, "The Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax" (1991)==.
- Translation and bilingualism both work.

**Evidence for the weak version**
- 🪝 **Guugu Yimithirr** (Australia): speakers say "an ant on your **north** leg" — they use compass directions instead of left and right, and keep track of direction at all times (Haviland; **Levinson 2003**).
- Colour-category boundaries affect how fast colours are told apart.

**Culture shaping language**
- Nuer cattle-colour terms; Hanunóo plant names.
- **Dravidian kin terms** mark cross-cousins (e.g. Telugu *maradalu*) as potential spouses, so the terminology encodes the marriage rule.
- Honorifics — *aap / tum / tu*, *meeru / nuvvu*.
- Loanwords — *pyjama* and *bungalow* into English; *school* and *ticket* into Hindi.
- Culture acts most on **vocabulary and pragmatics**, least on core syntax.

> **Verdict:** ==language is a lens, not a cage==. Influence runs both ways, within universal limits.

## 5. Linguistics ↔ anthropology; emic/etic (2015, 2019)

Language plays five roles in anthropology:
- **Subject** — linguistic anthropology is one of Boas's four fields.
- **Tool** — Malinowski (1922): do fieldwork in the native language.
- **Data** — kin, colour and plant terms (Morgan 1871; Conklin).
- **Model** — phonology gave Lévi-Strauss his structuralism; Geertz reads culture as a "text".
- **History** — language families and migrations; Swadesh's glottochronology (1950s).

🪝 *English hears the "p" in "pin" and "spin" as one sound; Hindi hears two — **phal** (fruit) and **pal** (moment).*
- **Phonetics** describes all sounds on a universal grid. **Phonemics** picks out the contrasts that change meaning in one language.
- ==**Pike (1954)** turned this into **etic** (the observer's comparative grid) and **emic** (the distinctions meaningful to insiders).== Harris later used the pair (6-G).

## 6. India

🪝 *January 2010: Boa Sr, about 85, died in Port Blair — the last speaker of **Bo**, a Great Andamanese language. For years there was no one left to speak it with, and she talked to birds instead.*

- **Language families:** Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Austroasiatic (Munda), Tibeto-Burman, plus Andamanese.
- **Census 2011:** 19,569 raw mother-tongue returns, rationalised to **121 languages**; **22** are in the Eighth Schedule.
- **People's Linguistic Survey of India** (Ganesh Devy): about **780** languages. It uses a different method from the Census.
- **NEP 2020:** teaching in the mother tongue "wherever possible" up to Grade 5.

## The traps

1. **FOXP2 ≠ "the language gene".**
2. Attack the **strong** Whorf hypothesis — not the weak one.
3. Cite "Eskimo snow words" **only as a myth**.
4. **Emic/etic is Pike's (1954)**, not Harris's.
5. **No language is "primitive"** (Boas).
6. For the origin of language, give a **range of dates**, not one date.
