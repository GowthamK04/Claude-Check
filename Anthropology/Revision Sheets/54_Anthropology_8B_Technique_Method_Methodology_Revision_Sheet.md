## 1. The distinction

🪝 *Cooking: the **technique** is how you chop; the **method** is the recipe; the **methodology** is your theory of good food — why this dish, and how you would know it is good.*

| Level | Definition | Answers | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Technique** | A specific, concrete tool or procedure for collecting or recording data | ==with what?== | Interview schedule, questionnaire, genealogy chart, map, photograph, pile sort |
| **Method** | A systematic strategy that combines techniques to answer a question | ==how?== | Participant observation, case-study method, genealogical method, comparative method, survey |
| **Methodology** | The logic and philosophy of inquiry — it justifies the choice of methods and defines valid knowledge | ==why, and how do we know?== | Positivism, interpretivism, phenomenology, pragmatism |

**Above and below the three levels**
- Above: **paradigm** (Kuhn 1962), **ontology** (what reality is) and **epistemology** (how it can be known).
- Below: **research design** — problem, questions, sampling, methods, analysis.

**How the levels relate**
- They are **hierarchical but not one-to-one**: one methodology can use many methods, and one technique (a questionnaire) can serve different methodologies.
- **Standard sources:** ==**Pelto & Pelto, *Anthropological Research: The Structure of Inquiry* (1970)**==; Bernard, *Research Methods in Anthropology* (1988 onward).

**Worked example — Malinowski in the Trobriands**

| Level | What it was |
|---|---|
| Methodology | Functionalist empiricism, inductive and holistic; the aim was the native's point of view |
| Method | Intensive participant observation |
| Techniques | Synoptic charts, genealogies, census, field diary, recorded native texts |

**Five methodological pairs**
- inductive / deductive
- qualitative / quantitative
- emic / etic (Pike 1954)
- idiographic / nomothetic (Windelband 1894)
- holistic / analytic

> **Answer line:** "Technique answers 'with what?', method 'how?', methodology 'why, and how do we know?' — and a good study is consistent across all three."

## 2. Positivist vs non-positivist (2007; 2025)

🪝 *Evans-Pritchard ran his household by the Azande **poison oracle**, and found it as satisfactory a way of managing his affairs as any he knew. He did not believe in witchcraft — he had learnt it from the inside.*

| Basis | Positivist — explain | Interpretive — understand |
|---|---|---|
| Reality | Objective | Socially constructed, meaningful |
| Aim | Causal laws (nomothetic) | Meaning (idiographic) |
| Logic | Deductive; hypothesis and test | Inductive; interpretation |
| Data | Measurable, often quantitative | Words, symbols, acts in context |
| Observer | Neutral, value-free | Positioned, reflexive |
| View | Etic | Emic |
| Names | ==Comte · **Durkheim, *Rules* (1895)**: treat social facts as things · Radcliffe-Brown's "natural science of society" · **Murdock**: HRAF (1937), *Social Structure* (1949) · White · Harris== | ==Dilthey · **Weber's *Verstehen*** · Boas · **Evans-Pritchard, Marett Lecture (1950)**: anthropology belongs with the humanities · **Geertz (1973)**: an interpretive science in search of meaning · phenomenology== |

**What each contributes, and its limits**
- **Positivist** — gives rigour, replicability and cross-cultural comparison, but loses context and meaning, makes a false claim to neutrality, and risks ethnocentric categories.
- **Interpretive** — gives meaning, the actor's view and reflexivity, but is subjective and hard to generalise.

**Verdict:** the two are ==complementary — anthropology **bridges science and the humanities**==. Mixed methods is the practical bridge.

## 3. Subjectivity (2006)

🪝 *Tepoztlán: **Redfield (1926)** saw harmony; **Lewis (1943)** saw conflict. Samoa: **Mead (1928)** saw an easy adolescence; **Freeman (1983)** said her informants had been joking. Same places, different methodologies.*

- **Where subjectivity enters:** the choice of problem and site; the researcher's gender, nationality, caste, class and theory; which informants are used; what gets noticed; interpretation; and the writing-up.
- **The observer effect:** people behave differently for the visitor.
- **Remedies:**
  - reflexivity
  - long residence
  - many informants
  - ==**triangulation (Denzin 1970)** — of data, investigators, theories and methods==
  - team and repeat studies
  - member checking (returning interpretations to the community)
  - transparent field notes
- **Subjectivity as a resource:** the experiential turn (Rosaldo 1989).
- **Intersubjectivity (Schutz):** knowledge is made *between* researcher and researched.

## 4. Phenomenology as a research method (2019)

🪝 *Paul Stoller's Songhay sorcerer-teacher in Niger told him to stop watching and learn with his body — through taste, sound and smell. Hence ***The Taste of Ethnographic Things*** (1989).*

**The philosophers**
- **Husserl:** "back to the things themselves"; ==**epoché** (bracketing your assumptions); the **lifeworld** (*Lebenswelt*)==.
- **Heidegger (1927):** being-in-the-world.
- **Schutz, *The Phenomenology of the Social World* (1932):** intersubjectivity; typifications.
- ==**Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945): the lived body**.==

**In anthropology**
- Michael Jackson, *Paths Toward a Clearing* (1989) — radical empiricism among the Kuranko.
- ==**Csordas, "Embodiment as a Paradigm for Anthropology" (1990)**== — linked to the 2025 note "Culture and embodiment".
- Stoller (1987, 1989).
- Kleinman, *The Illness Narratives* (1988).
- Bourdieu's habitus (1977).
- Gopal Guru, *Humiliation* (2009) — the lived experience of caste.

**As a method:** bracket your categories · learn through participant **experience** (skills, rituals, bodily techniques) · use open interviews and life narratives · attend to the senses, emotion, body, time and space.

**Used for:** illness and healing, pain, ritual and possession, migration, disability, caste humiliation.

**Critique:** subjectivism; weak generalisation; it neglects structure and power; true bracketing may be impossible; can experience really be shared across cultures?

## 5. Mixed methods (2026)

🪝 ***People of India*** (Anthropological Survey of India, led by K. S. Singh, 1985–92) profiled **4,635 communities** — one common schedule *and* one ethnographic account for each.

**Definition:** ==research that **combines and integrates** qualitative and quantitative data and methods== to answer a question better than either could alone (**Creswell & Plano Clark 2007**; Tashakkori & Teddlie 1998). Its philosophical basis is **pragmatism**.

**Why mix?**
- triangulation
- complementarity
- development (one strand builds the next)
- initiation (new questions arise)
- expansion

**The designs**

| Design | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Convergent parallel** | Quantitative and qualitative together, then merged | Household survey plus participant observation |
| **Explanatory sequential** | Quantitative first, then qualitative explains it | Census shows tribal drop-out → fieldwork finds why |
| **Exploratory sequential** | Qualitative first, then quantitative tests and scales it | Free-lists of illness terms → a district survey |
| **Embedded** | One strand inside the other | Interviews inside a nutrition trial |

**Examples to cite**
- **Lewis's Tepoztlán** (1951) — census, household budgets and Rorschach tests alongside fieldwork.
- **Rivers's genealogies** — counts that carry meaning.
- **People of India.**
- **Nutritional anthropology** (the 2025 question) — anthropometry plus the ethnography of food.
- **NFHS data** read alongside fieldwork.
- **PRA** evaluations.

**Limits:** clashing paradigms · cost, time and skills · **shallow integration** (two studies side by side) · the qualitative strand shrinks into mere illustration.

## 6. Comparisons

| Basis | Qualitative | Quantitative |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Meaning, process | Measurement, cause |
| Data | Words, observations | Numbers, scales |
| Sample | Small, purposive | Large, random |
| Tools | Participant observation, life history | Survey, census, anthropometry |

| Basis | Method | Methodology |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | A practical procedure | A philosophical justification |
| Question | How to collect? | Why this way — is it valid? |
| Example | Participant observation | The interpretivism that values it |

## The traps

1. **Method ≠ methodology** — practice vs philosophy.
2. **Participant observation is a method**; the field diary and genealogy chart are **techniques**.
3. **Mixed methods means integration**, not just using two tools.
4. **The restudies are about method**, not about which village was "right".
5. **Redfield's "enjoy / suffer" line** is reported second-hand — present it as reported.
