## 1. The big idea — four levels, each closer to the gene

A gene is a recipe for a protein, and a changed gene often makes a changed protein. The molecular methods trace that chain backwards, from the protein to the gene:
1. **Protein** — biochemical methods.
2. **Cell surface** — immunological methods (blood groups, tissue types).
3. **DNA itself** — DNA technology.
4. **Engineered DNA** — recombinant technology.

> **Exam line:** "Each level — protein, cell surface, DNA — is a step closer to the gene itself."

---

## 2. Biochemical methods — reading proteins

🪝 ***Garrod, 1902:** he studied babies whose nappies turned black (alkaptonuria). The condition was commoner in cousin marriages, and he realised one chemical step was missing. He called these conditions "**inborn errors of metabolism**".*

- ==**Electrophoresis:**== put a sample on a gel and pass an electric current through it. Proteins move according to their charge and size, so an unusual variant forms a band in a different place.

**Sickle-cell haemoglobin**

| Person | Bands on the gel |
|---|---|
| Normal | HbA only |
| ==Carrier== | ==**both** HbA and HbS== |
| Sickle-cell | HbS only |

🪝 ***Pauling, 1949:** he called sickle-cell the first "molecular disease". The entire difference is a single building block out of 146 in the haemoglobin chain.*

- **Enzyme tests:**
  - **G6PD deficiency** — causes anaemia after eating fava beans or taking some drugs, but protects against malaria.
  - **PKU** — newborn babies are screened so that a special diet can prevent brain damage.
- **Limit:** these methods only see genetic variants that change a protein.

## 3. Immunological methods — immunogenetics (2015)

🪝 ***First World War, Salonika:** the Hirszfelds typed the blood of thousands of soldiers and found that blood-group proportions differed from nation to nation — the first genetic comparison of human populations.*

- **Immunogenetics:** the genetics of the immune system and of the inherited molecules on our cells.
- **The basic reaction:** an ==**antigen**== (a molecule the immune system recognises) meets its ==**antibody**== (a protein made against it), and the cells clump together — ==**agglutination**==. Because antigens are inherited, this test reveals genetic differences.

**The main systems**
- **ABO** (==Landsteiner, 1900–01==): antigens A and B, controlled by three alleles. Mismatched blood clumps, which is why transfusions must be matched.
- **Rh** (1940): an Rh-negative mother carrying an Rh-positive baby can make antibodies that attack the baby's blood — haemolytic disease of the newborn. It is now prevented with an anti-D injection.
- **MN:** another blood group, used as an early population marker.
- **HLA:** tissue types on almost all cells. They decide transplant matching and are linked to some diseases — **HLA-B27** with ankylosing spondylitis.

**Uses**
- safe transfusion and transplants;
- preventing Rh disease;
- **paternity exclusion** (a child with group AB cannot have a group O father);
- studying links between genes and disease;
- **comparing populations** (see 9.6).

**Limit:** there are only a few such markers compared with DNA.

> **Exam line:** "Immunogenetics reads inherited antigens — ABO, Rh, MN, HLA — through antigen–antibody reactions, for transfusion, transplant, Rh disease, paternity exclusion and population comparison."

## 4. DNA technology

🪝 ***Leicester, September 1984:** Alec Jeffreys pulled a film from a developing tank and saw a barcode of bands that was different for every person except identical twins. Three years later the method caught a murderer and freed an innocent man.*

**Five steps**
1. **Extract** the DNA — from blood, saliva, hair or even ancient bone.
2. **Cut** it with ==**restriction enzymes**== ("molecular scissors").
3. **Copy** it by ==**PCR**==:
   - heat the DNA so its two strands separate;
   - cool it so short primers attach;
   - build new strands.

   Each cycle doubles the DNA, so about 30 cycles make roughly a billion copies (Mullis, 1983).
4. **Separate** the pieces on a gel.
5. **Read** the sequence of letters by **sequencing**.

**DNA fingerprinting**
- Some stretches of DNA repeat a short sequence over and over, and the ==number of repeats differs between people==. Cutting and separating these stretches gives each person a unique pattern. Today labs use **STRs** (short tandem repeats).
- **Uses:** crime, disaster victims, paternity, identifying remains.
- **India:** CCMB Hyderabad (Lalji Singh) developed it in the late 1980s.

**Markers:** RFLP, VNTR, STR and ==**SNP**== (a single-letter difference in DNA).

**Ancient DNA:** Pääbo (Nobel Prize, 2022) read the Neanderthal genome. People of non-African descent carry about 1–2% Neanderthal DNA.

## 5. Nuclear vs mitochondrial vs Y DNA (2025, 2026)

🪝 *The **Romanov** bones found in 1991 were identified by matching their mitochondrial DNA with Prince Philip's — he was related to the Tsarina through the female line.*

| Basis | Nuclear | Mitochondrial | Y chromosome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where | 46 chromosomes in the nucleus | A small ring in the mitochondria | Part of nuclear DNA |
| Size | ~3 billion letters | ~16,500 letters | ~57 million letters |
| Inherited from | Both parents | ==**Mother only**== | ==**Father → son only**== |
| Recombination | Yes, every generation | ==None== | Almost none |
| Copies per cell | Two | Hundreds or more | One |
| Mutation rate | Slower | ==Faster== | Moderate |
| Best for | Disease, mixture | ==Maternal line== | Paternal line |

**Why mtDNA is ideal for studying human history**
- it passes down only from mother to child;
- it is never reshuffled;
- it mutates fast enough to measure differences;
- there are many copies per cell, so it survives in old bones.

==**"Mitochondrial Eve" (1987):**== comparing mtDNA from people around the world traced all living humans to one female ancestor in **Africa**, about **200,000 years** ago. This strongly supports the **out-of-Africa** model. She was *not* the only woman alive — just the one whose maternal line happens to have survived.

**India:** mtDNA of the Andamanese (studied at CCMB) shows very ancient lineages, pointing to an early migration along the coast. DNA from a Harappan skeleton at Rakhigarhi was published in 2019.

> **Exam line:** "mtDNA — maternal, non-recombining, fast-mutating, abundant — traced living humans to an African 'Mitochondrial Eve' about 200,000 years ago."

## 6. Recombinant DNA technology (2000, 2001)

🪝 *Diabetics once relied on insulin extracted from slaughtered cattle and pigs. In 1978 the human insulin gene was put into bacteria, and in 1982 bacterial human insulin became the first genetically engineered medicine.*

- **What it is:** cutting DNA from one source and joining it to DNA from another, to make a combination that does not exist in nature (==Cohen & Boyer, 1973==).
- **The steps:**
  1. Cut out the gene with a restriction enzyme.
  2. Open a ==**plasmid**== (a small ring of bacterial DNA) with the same enzyme.
  3. Join the gene into it with ==**DNA ligase**==.
  4. Put the plasmid into *E. coli*.
  5. The bacteria multiply and copy the gene (==**cloning**==).
  6. Collect the human protein they make.
- **Uses:**
  - insulin, growth hormone and clotting factors;
  - the hepatitis B vaccine;
  - diagnosis;
  - **gene therapy**;
  - **CRISPR-Cas9** gene editing (2012; Nobel Prize 2020);
  - GM crops (Bt cotton in India, 2002).
- **Limits and ethics:**
  - safety and unintended effects;
  - **eugenics** — gene-edited babies were born in China in 2018;
  - cost and inequality;
  - ==**consent and ownership**== — the Havasupai tribe's blood samples were used without their consent.

> **Exam line:** "Recombinant DNA cuts a gene, joins it into a plasmid with ligase and clones it in bacteria — giving insulin, vaccines and gene therapy, but raising safety, eugenic, cost and consent concerns."

## The traps
1. Mitochondrial Eve was **not the only woman alive**.
2. mtDNA comes from the **mother only**; the Y chromosome passes **father → son**.
3. Electrophoresis **separates** molecules; PCR **copies** DNA.
4. A karyotype **can't see sickle-cell** — electrophoresis and DNA tests can.
