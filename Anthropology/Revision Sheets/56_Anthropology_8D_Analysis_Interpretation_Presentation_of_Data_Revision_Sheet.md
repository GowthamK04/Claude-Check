## 1. The big idea — four steps after collecting data

==**Sample → analyse → interpret → present.**==

1. **Sampling** decides *whose* story is told.
2. **Analysis** turns a mess into a pattern.
3. **Interpretation** turns the pattern into meaning.
4. **Presentation** decides who hears it.

In fieldwork the steps **loop back**: an early pattern sends you back to the field with new questions.

---

## 2. Sampling — choosing whom to study

🪝 *1936: the* Literary Digest *polled 2.4 million Americans, drawn from phone books and car registrations, and predicted Landon would beat Roosevelt. Roosevelt won by a landslide. Gallup, with about 50,000 carefully chosen people, got it right.* ==**How you choose matters more than how many.**==

**Three words to know**
- **Population:** the whole group you want to learn about.
- **Sample:** the part you actually study.
- **Sampling frame:** the list you pick from.

In villages there is often no list, so studies begin with a **village census** — going house to house and listing every household.

**Probability sampling** — everyone has a known chance of being picked, so you can generalise.

| Method | How it works | Use and weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple random** | A lottery | Fair, but needs a full list and may miss small groups |
| **Systematic** | Every *k*th unit after a random start. *Example:* 500 houses, a sample of 50; start at 7, then take 7, 17, 27… | Quick and even, but breaks if the list hides a pattern |
| **Stratified** | First split the population by caste, land or tribe, then sample randomly within each group | Guarantees a small Dalit or Adivasi group is represented. ==**Best for studying inequality.**== |
| **Cluster** | Pick whole villages or hamlets at random and study everyone in them | Saves travel; people within a cluster tend to be alike |
| **Multistage** | Narrow down in steps: district → village → household → person | How the **NFHS** works, since India has no single list of households |

**Non-probability sampling** — chosen deliberately; gives depth and access rather than generalisation.
- **Purposive:** people chosen for what they know — the priest, the midwife, the elder. This is how **key informants** are picked.
- **Snowball:** each person introduces the next. It is the only practical way to reach **hidden groups**, such as migrants, drug users and people living with HIV.
- **Quota:** fixed numbers per category, with no random selection.
- **Convenience:** whoever is easiest to reach — the weakest method.

**The usual field mix**
1. Census the whole small village.
2. Use a **stratified** sample of households for the schedule.
3. Choose key informants and cases **purposively**.
4. Follow migrants by **snowball**.
5. For a regional study, use **multistage**.

> **Rule:** the more you want to **generalise**, the more you need probability sampling; the more you want **depth** in one group, the more you rely on purposive choice.

🪝 *A well-drawn random sample of about a thousand can describe a country of over a billion, within a known margin of error.*

## 3. Analysis — turning a mess into a pattern

Imagine coming home with twelve notebooks, three hundred hours of tape and two hundred filled schedules. Analysis is how you turn that into something you can think with.
- **Organise:** write up rough jottings into full notes the same evening, transcribe interviews, and enter the schedules into tables.
- **Code:** tag each passage with a short label — "dowry", "land dispute", "migration".
- **Build themes:** group related codes into broader themes. For example, dowry, gold and wedding costs can become a theme about the rising cost of marriage.
- **Count where useful:** percentages, averages, and ==**cross-tabulation**== — setting two things against each other in one table, such as caste × landholding, to see whether they move together.
- ==**Grounded theory** (Glaser & Strauss, 1967):== build your categories **from the data upward**, rather than starting with a theory and testing it.

> **Exam line:** "Analysis organises raw data, codes it into themes and, where useful, counts it — often building categories from the data upward, as in grounded theory."

## 4. Interpretation — what does it all mean?

🪝 *Two boys close one eye. For one it is an involuntary twitch; for the other it is a wink — a secret signal. A camera records exactly the same movement. **Geertz:** the anthropologist's job is to tell the twitch from the wink.* This is ==**thick description**== — describing behaviour together with the meaning it has for the people involved.

- **Analysis vs interpretation:** analysis says *what* the pattern is; interpretation asks *why* and *what it means*.
  - *Example:* dowry is rising fastest where grooms hold government jobs. Is a government job now the most prized thing a groom can offer? How do families themselves explain it?
- Move between the **emic** view (people's own meaning) and the **etic** view (wider causes such as markets, law and history), and link your findings to theory.
- **How you test an interpretation:**
  - ==**Validity:** does it capture what is really there?==
  - ==**Reliability:** would another researcher, using the same method, find the same thing?==
- **Safeguards:**
  - triangulation;
  - looking for cases that contradict you;
  - returning your interpretations to the people studied;
  - being honest about your own position.
- **Traps to avoid:** **ethnocentrism** (judging by your own culture's standards) and over-generalising from one village.

> **Exam line:** "Interpretation turns patterns into meaning through thick description, moving between emic and etic views, tested for validity and reliability."

## 5. Presentation — telling the story

🪝 *1922: Malinowski's* Argonauts *(the book) and Flaherty's* Nanook of the North *(the film) appeared in the same year. And* Nanook *was partly staged — "Nanook" was really named Allakariallak, and an igloo was cut in half so the camera could film inside.*

- **Forms:** the monograph; tables, graphs and maps; genealogical charts; photographs, film and digital archives.
- **Ethics:**
  - change names and places (pseudonyms, anonymity);
  - get permission for photographs;
  - avoid anything that harms or shames a community;
  - ==**share findings back**== in a form people can use.

### How presentation changed — the 2020 question

| Basis | Classical monograph (1920s–60s) | Contemporary ethnography (1980s →) |
|---|---|---|
| Voice | Impersonal third person | **Reflexive** first person — the author's own position is shown |
| Authority | "I was there" | Shared, dialogic |
| People | Described, rarely quoted | ==**Polyphony**== — many voices, long quotations, life histories |
| Culture | One bounded "tribe" | Contested, changing, linked to the world |
| Time | The **ethnographic present** ("The Nuer herd cattle…"), as if frozen in time | History and change |
| Form | Standard chapters | Experimental: dialogues, multi-sited accounts, film, digital, co-authored |
| Readers | Scholars | Also the community |

**What changed it**
- the end of colonialism;
- Malinowski's private diary (1967), which showed the "objective observer" was a pose;
- feminist and native anthropologists asking whose voices were missing;
- ==***Writing Culture* (Clifford & Marcus, 1986)**== — ethnographic truths are always **partial**;
- postmodern doubt (6-J).

**Balance:** the classical style gave clarity and comparison; the new one gives honesty and voice, but can become self-absorbed. The best work keeps both.

> **Exam line:** "Presentation moved from the impersonal, authoritative, timeless monograph to reflexive, many-voiced, experimental ethnography — from the author alone to the author with the people."

## 6. The life table (2006 note)

🪝 *London, 1662: a shopkeeper, John Graunt, built the first life table from the weekly parish lists of burials.*

- **What it is:** follow an imaginary group of 1,000 (or 100,000) newborns, age by age, until all have died.
- **The columns:**
  - ==***l<sub>x</sub>*** — number still alive at each age==
  - ***d<sub>x</sub>*** — number who die during that age
  - ***q<sub>x</sub>*** — the chance of dying at that age
  - ==***e<sub>x</sub>*** — years of life still expected at that age==

  *e<sub>0</sub>* is life expectancy at birth.
- **Uses:** comparing the health of communities (for example, a tribe's higher infant deaths), and **palaeodemography** — estimating lifespans from ancient skeletons.
- **Limit:** it needs reliable records of births and deaths, which are often weak in remote areas.

## The traps
1. **Systematic** (every *k*th) ≠ **stratified** (split into groups first).
2. **Cluster** (whole groups) ≠ **stratified** (sample within every group).
3. **Validity** (is it true?) ≠ **reliability** (would it repeat?).
4. The 2020 question is about **writing**, not data tools.
5. Keep the life-table note **short and practical**.
