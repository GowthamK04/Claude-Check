## 1. Communication — words do social work

🪝 *"Nice weather today!" tells nobody anything new — yet silence would be an insult.* ==Malinowski (1923) called this **phatic communion**: talk that creates a bond rather than carrying information.==

- **Definition:** communication is the transmission of meaning through shared symbols and signals, verbal and non-verbal, within a cultural context.
- **Verbal** communication carries **content**. **Non-verbal** communication carries **relationship, emotion and status** — and when the two conflict, the non-verbal is usually believed.
- **Malinowski, "The Problem of Meaning in Primitive Languages" (1923):** meaning depends on the **context of situation**; language is a "mode of action".
- **Other codes:**
  - 🪝 **Silbo Gomero** — whistled Spanish on La Gomera; UNESCO heritage, 2009.
  - African talking drums.
  - **Sign languages** are full languages. India set up the ISLRTC in 2015.

## 2. Non-verbal channels (the 2017 note)

🪝 *Edward T. Hall watched an American diplomat keep stepping back while his Latin American colleague kept stepping closer — until the American was pinned against a wall. Each thought the other rude; the difference was culture.*

| Channel | What it covers | Scholar / example |
|---|---|---|
| **Kinesics** | gesture, posture, face | **Birdwhistell** coined it in 1952; *Kinesics and Context* (1970) |
| **Proxemics** | use of space | **Hall**, *The Silent Language* (1959); *The Hidden Dimension* (1966) |
| **Paralanguage** | tone, pitch, tempo, pause | **Trager** 1958 |
| **Haptics** | touch | touching elders' feet; *namaste* vs a handshake |
| **Chronemics** | use of time | Hall: **monochronic** vs **polychronic** cultures |
| **Oculesics** | gaze and its avoidance | lowered eyes before elders |
| **Artefacts** | dress, marks, ornaments | *sindoor*, sacred thread, tattoos |
| **Silence** | respect, refusal, grief | Western Apache (Basso 1970) |

==**Hall's four zones of space (1966):**==
- **intimate:** 0–45 cm
- **personal:** 45 cm–1.2 m
- **social:** 1.2–3.6 m
- **public:** 3.6 m and beyond

These are Hall's figures for middle-class Americans. The distances shrink in "contact cultures" (Arab, Latin American, Mediterranean). In historical Kerala, **distance pollution** — rules on how close lower castes could come — let caste fix physical space itself.

**Ekman & Friesen (1969) — five functions of non-verbal behaviour:**
- **emblems** — gestures with a direct verbal translation, like thumbs-up or *namaste*;
- **illustrators** — gestures that accompany speech;
- **affect displays** — emotion shown on the face;
- **regulators** — nods and glances that manage turn-taking;
- **adaptors** — self-touching and fidgeting.

## 3. Universal or cultural?

🪝 *Paul Ekman showed photographs of American faces to the Fore of Papua New Guinea, who had almost no contact with outsiders. They matched happiness, sadness, anger and disgust — but mixed up fear and surprise.*

- **The universal core:**
  - Darwin, *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872).
  - ==Ekman & Friesen (1971): **six basic expressions** — happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, disgust.==
- **The cultural rest:**
  - **Display rules** decide when an emotion may be shown — Japanese students masked disgust in front of an experimenter.
  - **Emblems** differ by culture — the Indian head-tilt means yes; a Bulgarian nod means no.
  - **Birdwhistell:** no gesture means the same thing everywhere.
- **Balance:** a small biological core of facial expressions, with culture deciding when, how much and to whom; most gestures, distances and touch rules are learnt.
- ⚠ The "**7–38–55**" rule (Mehrabian 1967) comes from small experiments on liking. **Do not quote it as a general law.**

## 4. Language in social context

🪝 *"I do" at a wedding doesn't describe a marriage — it makes one.* **Austin, *How to Do Things with Words* (1962):** such utterances are **performatives**; together these are **speech acts** (developed by Searle in 1969).

- **Hymes, ethnography of speaking (1962/64):** study when, where, by whom and how speech is used.
  - ==His **SPEAKING** grid (1972): **S**etting · **P**articipants · **E**nds · **A**ct sequence · **K**ey · **I**nstrumentalities · **N**orms · **G**enre.==
- ==**Communicative competence** (Hymes, against Chomsky): knowing **what to say, to whom, when and how** — not grammar alone.==
- **Speech community** (Gumperz): a group that shares norms for using and interpreting speech.
- **Register:** the variety suited to a setting — ritual, office or kitchen speech.
- **Code-switching:** moving between languages within one conversation.
  - 🪝 *Blom & Gumperz (1972), **Hemnesberget**, Norway: locals switched from dialect to standard when the talk turned official.*
  - Indian examples: Hinglish; Telugu–English in Hyderabad.
- ==**Diglossia** (Ferguson 1959):== a "high" variety for formal and written use and a "low" one for daily speech. Classic cases are Arabic, Swiss German and Greek; in India, **literary vs spoken Tamil**.
- **Pidgin and creole:** a pidgin has no native speakers; a creole is a pidgin learnt as a first language. **Nagamese** in Nagaland is a widely cited Indian case.
- **Honorifics:** Hindi *aap / tum / tu*; Telugu *meeru / nuvvu*; Javanese speech levels (Geertz, *The Religion of Java*, 1960).

| | Code-switching | Diglossia |
|---|---|---|
| Level | individual, within one conversation | societal, by domain |
| Varieties | any two languages or dialects | a high and a low form of one language |
| Choice | strategic, fluid | fixed by function |
| Case | Hinglish; Hemnesberget | Tamil; Arabic |

## 5. Branches of linguistic anthropology (2021)

1. **Descriptive or structural linguistics** — Boas, Sapir, Bloomfield.
2. **Historical linguistics** — Sir William Jones (1786); Swadesh's glottochronology.
3. **Ethnolinguistics** — Whorf, Conklin, Frake.
4. **Sociolinguistics** — Labov, Gumperz, Ferguson.
5. **Ethnography of communication** — Hymes, Gumperz.

**Duranti (2003), *Current Anthropology*:** the field has had three paradigms — (1) Boasian documentation of languages → (2) ethnography of communication → (3) language as social action (identity, power, ideology).

## 6. Variation and inequality — the 2020 question

🪝 *Labov, 1962: "Excuse me, where are the women's shoes?" — "Fourth floor." The **r** in "fourth floor" was pronounced by roughly **6 in 10** staff at upmarket Saks, **5 in 10** at Macy's, and **2 in 10** at cheap S. Klein.* One consonant tracked social class.

🪝 *John Gumperz in **Khalapur** (Uttar Pradesh), 1950s: everyone spoke "the village dialect", yet each caste spoke its own version — and the sharpest differences ran between touchable and untouchable castes.*

**Patterns of variation**
- **Class** — Labov, *The Social Stratification of English in New York City* (1966). Speakers **shift style** towards prestige forms when they are being careful.
- **Caste** — ==**Gumperz 1958**, Khalapur==; Bright & Ramanujan (1964) on Brahmin vs non-Brahmin dialects.
- **Gender** — Lakoff, *Language and Woman's Place* (1975); Tannen (1990). In India, many communities have taboos on a wife saying her husband's name.
- **Region and ethnicity** — standard Hindi ranked over Bhojpuri; standard English over African American English.

**The mechanism: variation → evaluation → access → reproduction**

| Step | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Variation** | Groups speak differently | Labov; Gumperz |
| **Evaluation** | A **language ideology** labels one variety "correct" and others crude | Silverstein; Irvine & Gal 2000 |
| **Access** | Schools reward the middle-class **elaborated code** over the working-class **restricted code** | ==**Bernstein, *Class, Codes and Control* (1971)**== |
| | The "legitimate" variety works as **linguistic capital** | ==**Bourdieu, *Language and Symbolic Power* (1991)**== |
| | Interviews, courts and offices misread minority speech styles (gatekeeping) | Gumperz, *Discourse Strategies* (1982) |
| **Reproduction** | The hierarchy passes to the next generation | — |

**The counter-argument**

==**Labov, "The Logic of Nonstandard English" (1969): difference, not deficit.**== Stigmatised varieties are fully rule-governed. Bernstein was *read* as a deficit theorist, though he denied it.

**India**
- English works as elite **linguistic capital**.
- Tribal children taught only in a regional standard they do not speak fall behind.
- Odisha's multilingual education in tribal languages (from the late 2000s) is one response.
- ==**Article 350A**== provides for primary education in the mother tongue for linguistic minorities; **NEP 2020** asks for the mother tongue "wherever possible" up to Grade 5.
- Only **22** languages are in the Eighth Schedule.
- Srinivas noted that Sanskritisation includes adopting "purer" speech.

> **Answer line:** "Variation becomes inequality when one variety is made the price of entry to school, work and law."

## The traps

1. **Code-switching ≠ diglossia** — individual and fluid vs societal and fixed by domain.
2. Ekman's universals cover **a few facial expressions**, not gestures.
3. **Do not quote 7–38–55** as a law.
4. Bernstein was ***read*** as a deficit theorist — say it that way.
5. Hall's distances describe **one culture**; present them as an illustration of variation.
6. Labov's percentages are **approximate** — write "about 6, 5 and 2 in 10".
