## 1. The big idea — five ways of knowing

Every tool answers a different question:
- **Watching** shows what people *do*.
- **Asking** shows what they *say and think*.
- **Tracing** shows how things *came to be*.
- **Reading** shows what *others recorded before you*.
- **Sharing** shows how people *see their own world*.

==What people say and what they do are often different — which is why no single tool is enough.== Using several tools on the same question is **triangulation**, and it is how impressions become evidence.

## 2. Watch — observation

🪝 *The Hawthorne factory, 1920s: researchers made the lights brighter, then dimmer, then changed the breaks — and the workers' output rose every time. The real reason was that they knew they were being watched.* This is the **observer effect**.

- **Why observe?** Ask a farmer what he gives the temple and he may name a proud round number. Spend a harvest beside him and you see what he really gives. Observation catches behaviour as it happens, not as it is remembered.
- **Participant observation:** you live among the people, take part in daily life, and record it carefully.
  - It reveals the everyday details no one would think to tell you. **Malinowski** called these the "imponderabilia of actual life".
  - Over months, people stop performing for you.
- **Non-participant observation:** you watch from the side — a market, a court, a classroom. It is quicker and disturbs less, but you see *what* happened without knowing *why*.
- ==**The four observer roles**, from closest to most distant:==
  1. **Complete participant** — fully a member; the research may be hidden, which is an ethical risk.
  2. **Participant-as-observer** — openly a researcher but deeply involved. This is the usual anthropological role.
  3. **Observer-as-participant** — light, brief involvement.
  4. **Complete observer** — watches only.
- **Limits:**
  - it is slow;
  - it depends on one person's eyes (two anthropologists can see the same village differently);
  - the observer effect;
  - it covers a small scale, so findings are hard to generalise;
  - there is the danger of **"going native"** — losing critical distance.

  These limits don't make it useless; they mean it must be cross-checked.

> **Exam line:** "Participant observation captures what people do rather than what they say — but its depth is bought at the cost of objectivity and generalisation, so it must be triangulated."

## 3. Ask — interviews, schedules, questionnaires

🪝 *Evans-Pritchard trying to interview a Nuer man: "Who are you?" — "A man." "What is your name?" — "Do you want to know my name?"… He joked that he had caught "Nuerosis".* People control what outsiders learn, so interviewing depends on trust.

**Interviews**
- **Structured:** the same questions in the same order, so answers are easy to compare.
- **Semi-structured:** a list of topics, with freedom to follow where the talk goes.
- **Open:** close to a free conversation. It is slow, but it is where the surprises come out.
- **Key informants:** especially knowledgeable people — an elder, a priest, a midwife. They act as the fieldworker's teachers.

**Schedule vs questionnaire.** ==The one difference that explains all the others is **who holds the pen**.==

| Basis | Schedule | Questionnaire |
|---|---|---|
| Filled by | **the researcher**, face to face | **the respondent**, alone |
| Literacy needed | no | yes |
| Response rate | high; questions can be explained | often low; no one to explain |
| Cost and reach | slow, costly, small area | cheap, fast, wide area |
| Risk | the interviewer influences answers | questions misread |
| Best for | rural and tribal India | literate, urban, dispersed groups |

🪝 *Census 2011: about 27 lakh enumerators filled in forms door to door.* So the Census is a giant **schedule**, which is why it works where many people cannot read.

**Why not rely on forms alone?** A form only records what it thinks to ask. Forms give breadth, observation gives meaning — use both.

> **Exam line:** "The schedule is filled by the researcher and suits non-literate rural populations; the questionnaire is filled by the respondent and suits literate, dispersed ones."

## 4. Trace — case study, genealogy, life history, oral history

### Case study
🪝 *1938, Zululand: **Max Gluckman** described the opening of one new bridge — who stood where, who spoke, who ate with whom, who was kept apart. Through that single day he showed the whole structure of colonial society.*

- **What it is:** a close study of one unit — a family, an event, a dispute or a village — in its full context.
- **Why it helps:** it shows ==the **gap between rule and practice**==.
  - *Example:* the rule says sons share land equally. A case study of one family's quarrel shows the eldest son claiming more for caring for the parents, a daughter pressing a legal claim, and a caste panchayat stepping in.
- ==**The extended case method** (Gluckman):== follow the same people and disputes for months or years. Society then appears as a **process** of conflict and change, not a fixed structure.
- **Limit:** one case may be unusual. Choose cases carefully (a typical or an extreme one) and set them against wider data such as a village census.

> **Exam line:** "A case study examines one unit in depth; through the extended case method it reveals social life as process and exposes the gap between rule and practice."

### Genealogy
🪝 *1898, the Torres Strait: **W. H. R. Rivers** couldn't tell who was related to whom, so he asked each person "Who is your father? your mother? your children?" and drew the answers as a chart. The chart revealed marriage rules, inheritance, residence — a whole social system.*

- ==**The genealogical method** (Rivers, fully set out in 1910):== collect each person's relatives as a chart drawn with standard symbols.
- **Why it matters:** in small societies almost everything runs through kinship. Lay other information over the chart — who married whom, where couples live, how land and titles pass, which relatives share a kin term — and it becomes **a map of social structure**.
- **A practical bonus:** it gives the fieldworker a reason to talk to everyone, and a way to remember who is who.
- **Limit:** memory and politics. People forget ancestors, claim powerful ones, and leave out relatives they are ashamed of.

**Pedigree vs genealogy (2025)**

| | Genealogy | Pedigree |
|---|---|---|
| Kind of chart | **social** | **biological** (human genetics) |
| What it records | relatives as people themselves recognise them, in their own kin terms, including in-laws and adoptees | **one inherited trait** (sickle-cell, haemophilia, colour blindness), shown by shaded symbols |
| Purpose | to understand marriage, descent and inheritance | to work out how the trait is inherited |

==A pedigree asks **which gene passed to whom**; a genealogy asks **who counts as whose relative, and what follows**.==

### Life history and oral history
- **Life history:** one person's life, told over many sessions.
  - *Example:* an old Gond woman describing her marriage, the fencing of the forest, the first school, the dam. One life becomes a window on how a community lived through change.
  - It gives voice to people that official records ignore.
- **Oral history:** a **group's** remembered past — migrations, famines, how the village was founded — gathered from many elders. It is the archive of societies without written records.
- 🪝 *India's Forest Rights Act rules accept **statements of village elders** as evidence of a community's use of a forest.* Oral history can stand in a legal claim.
- **Limit:** memory is selective. Cross-check accounts against each other and against documents. Treat *what* a group chooses to remember as data too.

> **Exam line:** "Life history follows one life to reveal change from within; oral history gathers a group's remembered past — both give voice to the unrecorded but must be cross-checked."

## 5. Read — secondary sources
- **What they are:** the Census, district gazetteers, land and court records, government reports, colonial ethnographies, newspapers and photographs.
- **What they give:** history, scale, and a baseline against which to measure change.
- **The catch:** they were made **for someone else's purpose**. Colonial lists of "criminal tribes", for example, classified people in order to govern them.
- **So read them critically.** Ask: who made this, why, and whom does it leave out?

## 6. Share — participatory methods (PRA)

🪝 *In PRA the outsider **hands over the stick**. Villagers draw their own village on the ground with chalk, coloured powder, seeds and stones — and argue over it until it is right.*

- **Why it arose:** experts visited villages for a few days and made plans that failed, because they never understood local life.
- ==**Robert Chambers** ("putting the last first")== helped develop **Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA)**. Local people gather and analyse their own information, and the outsider only facilitates. NGOs in Karnataka were early pioneers.
- **The tools:**
  - **social map** — every house, often marked with caste or wealth;
  - **resource map** — land, water, forest, grazing;
  - **seasonal calendar** — work, hunger, illness and festivals through the year;
  - **transect walk** — walking across the village, noting soils and problems;
  - **wealth ranking** — households sorted by the villagers' own criteria;
  - **Venn diagram** — which institutions matter to people.
- **Strengths:** it uses local knowledge, gives people ownership of the findings, is quick and cheap, and suits development work.
- **Limits:**
  - a few days are not long fieldwork;
  - men and dominant castes can dominate the exercises;
  - "participation" can turn into box-ticking.
- **Naming:** the 2004 question wrote "Participatory *Rapid* Assessment". The standard name is Participatory *Rural* Appraisal, which grew out of Rapid Rural Appraisal. Mention both.

## 8. Comparisons

| | Participant | Non-participant |
|---|---|---|
| Involvement | lives in, takes part | watches from outside |
| What it gets | meaning, the unspoken | surface behaviour |
| Main risk | bias, going native | misreading without context |

| | Life history | Oral history |
|---|---|---|
| Unit | one person | a group's past |
| Best for | change seen from inside a life | the history of non-literate groups |

## The traps
1. The Census is a **schedule**, not a questionnaire.
2. Genealogy (social) ≠ pedigree (biological).
3. Life history (one person) ≠ oral history (a group).
4. PRA = Participatory **Rural** Appraisal.
5. Every "evaluate" answer needs **limits** and **triangulation**.
