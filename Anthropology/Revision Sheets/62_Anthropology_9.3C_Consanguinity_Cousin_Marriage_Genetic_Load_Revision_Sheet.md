## 1. What consanguinity is

🪝 *Darwin married his first cousin, Emma Wedgwood. Three of their ten children died young, and in 1870 he asked Parliament to count cousin marriages in the census. It refused.*

- ==**Consanguineous marriage:** the partners share a recent common ancestor — conventionally **second cousins or closer**.== A **non-consanguineous** marriage has no such link.
- ==**Consanguinity** is the **relationship**; **inbreeding** is its **genetic effect** — more homozygosity in the children or the population.== One is the cause, the other the measurable result.

## 2. Why relatives' children are at higher risk

- Everyone carries a few hidden harmful recessive alleles.
- **Unrelated** partners usually hide *different* ones, so their children get at least one normal copy.
- **Relatives** share grandparents, so they may hide the *same* allele. Their child can then receive two copies from one ancestor — copies that are ==**identical by descent**==.
- ==**Coefficient of inbreeding (F):** the chance that a child's two copies of a gene are identical by descent.==

| Relationship | F |
|---|---|
| brother–sister, parent–child | **1/4** |
| uncle–niece, double first cousins | **1/8** |
| ==**first cousins**== | ==**1/16**== |
| first cousins once removed | 1/32 |
| second cousins | 1/64 |

## 3. How much does the risk rise?

**Worked example — a recessive that affects 1 in 10,000 children of unrelated parents (q = 0.01):**
- ==Risk for first cousins' child = **q² + F·p·q** = 0.0001 + (1/16 × 0.99 × 0.01) ≈ 0.0007==
- That is **about 1 in 1,400 — roughly seven times** the usual risk.
- **The rarer the recessive, the bigger this multiplier**, and the larger the share of affected children born to related parents.

**Overall risk:**
- ==Serious birth defects run at about **2–3%** for unrelated parents and roughly **4–6%** for first cousins.==
- So the risk roughly doubles — but more than 9 in 10 children of cousins are still healthy.

## 4. Effects of consanguinity — the balanced view

| Harmful | Neutral or beneficial |
|---|---|
| more recessive disorders (thalassaemia, sickle-cell, albinism, metabolic disorders) | most children are healthy; risk rises only a few points |
| more malformations and infant deaths | **purging** — long inbreeding exposes harmful alleles, and selection removes them |
| **inbreeding depression** (slightly lower birth weight, poorer health) | social benefits: land stays in the family, safety for daughters, kin support, smaller dowry |
| combined with endogamy, it concentrates disorders specific to a community | often slightly higher fertility |

🪝 ***Bittles:** over a billion people live where marrying a relative is preferred; worldwide, about 1 marriage in 10 is between second cousins or closer.*

**India**
- **Cross-cousin** and **uncle–niece** marriage are customary in south India (part of Dravidian kinship, see 2.5).
- **Parallel-cousin** marriage is common in many Muslim communities.
- ==The **Hindu Marriage Act 1955** bans marriage between close (sapinda) relatives **unless custom permits it**.==
- Rates are falling with education and city life.

**The answer is not a ban** — it is **carrier screening and genetic counselling** (9.4).

## 5. Marriage rules and the gene pool (2020)

**Why ask an anthropologist?** Marriage rules decide who has children with whom, so they decide how genes move through a population.

| Rule | Genetic consequence |
|---|---|
| **Endogamy** (marrying within the group) | separate gene pools; drift and founder disorders |
| **Exogamy** (marrying outside the clan, gotra or village) | mixing within the endogamous group |
| **Cousin / uncle–niece** marriage | more homozygotes; more recessive disorders |
| **Hypergamy** (women marrying into higher groups) | one-way gene flow upward |
| **Polygyny** (one man, several wives) | fewer fathers, so a smaller effective population and stronger drift |
| **Levirate / sororate** (marrying a dead spouse's sibling) | reproduction kept within the same lineages |
| **Assortative mating** (choosing similar partners) | traits sorted; groups become more uniform |
| **Rules weakening** (inter-caste, urban, migrant marriage) | more gene flow, less inbreeding |

> **Line:** ==kinship rules are genetic rules in disguise.==

## 6. Genetic load

🪝 ***Muller** (1950), "Our Load of Mutations": every population carries a hidden burden of harmful genes — and radiation from nuclear tests was adding to it.*

- ==**Genetic load:** the loss of a population's average fitness compared with the best possible genotype== — in other words, the extra deaths, disease and infertility caused by harmful alleles.
- **Six types:**
  - **mutational** — new harmful mutations keep arising;
  - ==**segregational (balanced)** — the cost of a balanced polymorphism, e.g. the SS children that the sickle-cell balance inevitably produces;==
  - **inbreeding** — hidden recessives paired up;
  - **drift** — harmful alleles made common by chance;
  - **migration** — alleles brought in that don't suit the new environment;
  - **incompatibility** — e.g. an Rh-negative mother whose antibodies attack an Rh-positive foetus.
- ==**How it is measured:** compare deaths and disease in the children of related vs unrelated parents, against F. Morton, Crow & Muller (1956) expressed the result as **lethal equivalents** — roughly one to a few per person.==
- **What raises load:**
  - the mutation rate (radiation, chemicals);
  - inbreeding;
  - small, isolated populations;
  - balancing selection;
  - **relaxed selection** — medicine keeps people with genetic disorders alive to have children.
- **What lowers load:** outbreeding and better health.

## The traps
1. First cousins have **F = 1/16**, not 1/8 (1/8 is uncle–niece).
2. **Consanguinity** is the relationship; **inbreeding** is the genetic effect.
3. Cousin marriage **roughly doubles** the background risk — it does not make a disorder likely.
4. **Segregational load** comes from balanced polymorphism, not from inbreeding.
