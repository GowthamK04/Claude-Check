## 1. The big idea — why humans need special methods

We cannot breed people the way Mendel bred peas. Human families are small, each generation takes about 25 years, and families share homes as well as genes. So human genetics uses **natural experiments** — situations life has already arranged:
- families show the **pattern** of inheritance;
- twins and adoptees **separate genes from environment**;
- the karyotype lets us **see chromosomes** directly.

> **Exam line:** "Because humans cannot be bred like peas, human genetics relies on natural experiments — families, twins and adoptees."

## 2. Family study and pedigree analysis

🪝 *Queen Victoria carried the gene for haemophilia (blood that fails to clot). Through her daughters it spread into the royal families of Spain, Germany and Russia — reaching Alexei, the last Tsar's only son. It is the classic textbook pedigree.*

- **Family study:** compare how often a trait appears among relatives of patients with how often it appears in the general population. Much more often in relatives suggests it is inherited.
- **Pedigree:** a family chart drawn with standard symbols.
  - Square = male; circle = female.
  - Filled = affected; dot = carrier.
  - The **proband** is the person through whom the family first came to notice.
- ==**Reading the four patterns:**==

| Pattern | The clue | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Autosomal dominant** | Appears in **every generation**, in both sexes; an affected parent passes it to about half the children | Huntington's disease, achondroplasia |
| **Autosomal recessive** | **Skips generations**; two healthy carrier parents can have an affected child; commoner when relatives marry | Sickle-cell anaemia, thalassaemia, albinism |
| **X-linked recessive** | Mostly **males** are affected; passed through **carrier mothers**; **never father → son** (a father gives his son a Y, not an X) | Haemophilia, colour blindness, Duchenne muscular dystrophy |
| **X-linked dominant** | An affected father passes it to **all his daughters** and none of his sons | Vitamin-D-resistant rickets |

- **Limits:**
  - small families;
  - missing records;
  - **penetrance** — a gene may not show itself at all;
  - **expressivity** — a gene may show mildly or strongly;
  - new mutations;
  - shared environment.

> **Exam line:** "Pedigree analysis reveals whether a trait is autosomal or X-linked, dominant or recessive — but small families and shared environments limit what it can prove."

## 3. Twin studies

🪝 *Two identical twins were separated at birth and both named Jim. Each married a Linda, then a Betty; one named his son James Alan, the other James Allan; both had a dog called Toy.* Much of this is coincidence — but it is a vivid version of the genes-versus-environment question.

- ==**Galton (1875)** first used twins this way and coined the phrase "nature and nurture".==
- ==**Monozygotic (MZ), or identical, twins**== come from one egg that splits.
  - They share about **100%** of their genes and are always the same sex.
  - They occur at about 3–4 per 1,000 births, everywhere in the world.
- ==**Dizygotic (DZ), or fraternal, twins**== come from two eggs fertilised by two sperm.
  - They share about **50%** of their genes, like ordinary siblings.
  - Their rate varies: it is very high among the Yoruba of Nigeria, and rises with the mother's age and with IVF.

**The logic**
- Both kinds of twins usually grow up in the same home. So if identical twins are much more alike than fraternal twins, **genes** must be the reason.
- The measure is ==**concordance**== — the percentage of pairs in which, if one twin has the trait, the other has it too.
- If MZ concordance is much higher than DZ, genes matter. If MZ concordance is still below 100%, environment matters too. (Schizophrenia shows both.)
- ==**Heritability** (Holzinger): H = (C<sub>MZ</sub> − C<sub>DZ</sub>) ÷ (100 − C<sub>DZ</sub>).== It is the share of variation *in a population* that is due to genes.

**Diagnosing zygosity** (are the twins identical or fraternal?)
- Opposite-sex twins must be fraternal.
- For same-sex twins, look at the placenta and membranes at birth, physical features, blood groups and fingerprints — today, **DNA** settles it.
- **Weinberg's rule**, for a whole population: fraternal pairs ≈ 2 × the number of opposite-sex pairs.

**Nature–nurture (1999).** **Identical twins reared apart** share genes but not a home, so the similarities between them cannot come from a shared upbringing. They turn out to be strikingly alike in height and test scores — yet identical genes do not produce identical lives.

**Limits of twin studies**
- Identical twins are often treated more alike than fraternal twins (the "equal environment" assumption fails).
- Twins share a womb.
- Identical twins drift apart over life through **epigenetics** (changes in how genes are switched on).
- Heritability describes a population, not how "genetic" one person is.

> **Exam line:** "Comparing MZ and DZ concordance estimates heritability — but twin data show influence, not destiny."

## 4. Foster-child and co-twin methods

**Foster-child (adoption) method**
- An adopted child gets genes from the **birth parents** and a home from the **adoptive parents**. If adoptees resemble their birth parents, that points to genes; if they resemble their adoptive family, it points to environment.
- *Example:* the **Danish adoption studies** of the 1960s–70s found that children of parents with schizophrenia, adopted into other families, still developed it more often than other adoptees.
- **Limits:**
  - **selective placement** — agencies tend to match children to similar families;
  - the months in the womb are an environment too;
  - children adopted late have already lived in their first home.

🪝 ***Gesell, 1929:** one identical twin girl got six weeks of daily stair-climbing practice; her sister got two weeks of practice later — and soon caught up.* Training helped, but readiness mattered more.

**Co-twin control method**
- One identical twin gets a treatment, diet or training; the other does not. They have the same genes, so any difference is caused by the treatment.
- **Limits:** few pairs are available, results may not apply to everyone, and withholding a treatment from one twin raises ethical questions.

## 5. Karyotype — seeing the chromosomes (2024)

🪝 *For about 30 years, textbooks said humans have 48 chromosomes. In 1956 **Tjio & Levan** showed the true number is 46. In 1959 **Lejeune** found that people with Down syndrome have an extra chromosome 21 — the first disorder ever linked to a chromosome.*

- ==**Karyotype:**== a picture of all a person's chromosomes, arranged in pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes plus XX or XY. It is written ==**46,XX**== or ==**46,XY**==.

**How a karyotype is made — six steps**
1. **Blood** — white blood cells are taken.
2. **Culture** — the cells are grown for about 3 days and made to divide.
3. **Colchicine** — division is stopped at ==**metaphase**==, when chromosomes are most visible.
4. **Spread and fix** — a weak salt solution swells the cells so the chromosomes spread out; they are dropped onto a slide.
5. **Stain** — Giemsa stain gives each chromosome its own light and dark **bands**.
6. **Arrange** — the chromosomes are lined up in 23 pairs by size and centromere position, in groups A–G, with the sex chromosomes last.

**What it diagnoses**

| Syndrome | Karyotype | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Down** | ==47,+21== | Intellectual disability |
| Edwards / Patau | +18 / +13 | Severe; early death |
| **Turner** | ==45,X== | Girls; short stature, infertile |
| **Klinefelter** | ==47,XXY== | Boys; infertile |
| Cri-du-chat | Part of 5p deleted | Cat-like cry |
| Philadelphia chromosome | Translocation between 9 and 22 | Leukaemia |

- **Uses:** birth defects, developmental delay, repeated miscarriage, infertility, cancer, and **prenatal diagnosis** (amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling) followed by counselling.
- **Limit:** it ==cannot see single-gene disorders== such as sickle-cell — those need the 9.1-B methods.
- **India:** prenatal testing was misused to find the sex of the fetus, which led to the **PCPNDT Act** (1994), banning sex determination.

> **Exam line:** "A karyotype arranges the 46 chromosomes after culture, metaphase arrest and banding, diagnosing Down (47,+21), Turner (45,X) and Klinefelter (47,XXY) — but not single-gene disorders."

## 6. Putting the methods together (2026)

To show a disease is inherited, and how, work through these in order:
1. **Family study** — is it commoner in relatives?
2. **Pedigree** — which pattern does it follow?
3. **Twins** — is MZ concordance much higher than DZ?
4. **Adoptees** — does it follow the biological parents?
5. **Karyotype** — is a whole chromosome involved?
6. **Molecular tests** (9.1-B) — if not, find the gene.

==When all of these agree, heredity is proved.==

## The traps
1. A **pedigree** (biological, one trait) ≠ a **genealogy** (social — see 8-C).
2. Heritability ≠ how "genetic" one person is.
3. X-linked recessive traits are **never passed father to son**.
4. A karyotype **cannot see sickle-cell**.
