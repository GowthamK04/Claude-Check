## 1. Mendel's rules in plain words

🪝 *Mendel's pea paper (1865) lay unread for 35 years. In 1902 **Garrod** noticed that "black urine" (alkaptonuria) ran in cousin-married families — exactly the pattern Mendel predicted for a recessive factor. It was the first human recessive trait.*

**The vocabulary**
- Each trait is controlled by a **pair** of instructions, one from each parent.
- The versions of a gene are **alleles**; the gene's place on the chromosome is its **locus**.
- Two identical copies = **homozygous**; two different copies = **heterozygous**.
- The pair you carry is your **genotype**; what shows is your **phenotype**.
- The allele that shows in a heterozygote is **dominant**; the hidden one is **recessive**.

**The two laws**
- ==**Law of segregation:** the two copies separate when eggs and sperm form, so each child gets one at random.== This is why recessive traits can skip generations.
- ==**Law of independent assortment:** copies of different genes are sorted independently== — except **linked** genes that sit close together on a chromosome.

**Two carriers (Aa × Aa)** give genotypes in a ==1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa== ratio, which means ==3 normal : 1 affected==.

**Why humans are hard to study:** there are no planned crosses, families are small, and generations are long. So geneticists read the crosses families have already made, using the **pedigree** and twin studies (9.1-A).

## 2. One gene — the family patterns

🪝 *Queen Victoria carried haemophilia on one X chromosome; through her daughters it spread to the royal houses of Spain, Germany and Russia. Romanov DNA (2009) confirmed the mutation.*

| Pattern | Clues | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Autosomal dominant** | every generation · an affected parent · 1 in 2 risk per child | Huntington's, achondroplasia, polydactyly |
| **Autosomal recessive** | skips generations · carrier parents · 1 in 4 risk · commoner in cousin marriages | albinism, sickle-cell, β-thalassaemia, PKU |
| **X-linked recessive** | mostly males · carrier mothers · ==**never father → son**== | haemophilia, colour blindness, DMD, G6PD |
| **X-linked dominant** | an affected father → **all daughters, no sons** | vitamin D-resistant rickets |
| **Y-linked** | father → all sons only | SRY ("hairy ears" is now doubted) |

**Still single-gene, though not simple dominance:**
- **Incomplete dominance** — the heterozygote is in between.
- ==**Codominance** — both alleles show fully== (blood group AB).
- **Multiple alleles** — more than two versions in a population (A, B, O).

## 3. Lethal and sub-lethal genes

🪝 *Cuénot (1905) crossed yellow mice with yellow mice and got **2 yellow : 1 grey**, not 3:1. The YY quarter died in the womb.* ==A **2:1 ratio** is the signature of a recessive lethal.==

| Category | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Lethal** | death before reproduction | Tay–Sachs (death by about age 4); the yellow mice |
| **Sub-lethal** | kills some, or shortens life | thalassaemia major, sickle-cell anaemia, haemophilia (before treatment) |
| **Sub-vital** | slightly lower fitness | — |
| **Vital** | normal | — |
| **Conditional** | harmful only in certain conditions | G6PD deficiency + fava beans or some antimalarials |

- **Dominant lethals** vanish within a generation, unless they act late — like **Huntington's**, which strikes after people have had children.
- "Lethal" depends partly on medicine: transfusions make thalassaemia manageable.
- ==**Why lethal genes persist:**==
  1. they hide in healthy **carriers**;
  2. **mutation** keeps creating them;
  3. carriers sometimes gain an **advantage** (sickle-cell vs malaria → 9.3-A).
- 🪝 *India's **Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission** (2023) aims to screen about 7 crore people in tribal areas and eliminate the disease by 2047 — applied Mendelian genetics.*

## 4. Polygenic and multifactorial inheritance

**Why isn't height simply "tall" or "short"?** Hundreds of genes each add a little to it. The result is **continuous** variation that forms a **bell curve**. The genes' effects simply add up — they are **additive**.

- **Two-gene model:** a child can get 0 to 4 "dark" alleles, giving five shades in a ==1 : 4 : 6 : 4 : 1== ratio. Add more genes and the steps blur into a smooth curve.
- ==**Fisher (1918):** many Mendelian genes acting together produce continuous variation.== This ended the quarrel between the Mendelians and those who studied continuous traits.
- ==**Multifactorial:** genes plus environment (diet, lifestyle, conditions in the womb) set a "liability"; disease appears only past a **threshold**.==
  - Examples: type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cleft lip, heart disease.
  - Such conditions run in families but without neat ratios; relatives are at higher risk; lifestyle changes the risk.
- 🪝 *More than ten thousand DNA variants are linked to height, yet Indians have grown several centimetres taller in a few generations.* That is the environment acting on the same genes.

| | Monogenic | Polygenic / multifactorial |
|---|---|---|
| Genes | one | many, additive (plus environment) |
| Variation | discrete classes | continuous bell curve |
| Family pattern | clear ratios | loose |
| Environment | little effect | large effect |
| Examples | albinism, haemophilia, ABO | stature, skin colour, diabetes |

> **Line:** ==One gene gives kinds; many genes give degrees; genes plus environment give risk.==

## 5. Non-Mendelian patterns, and the limits of Mendel in man

**Patterns that break Mendel's rules**
- **Mitochondrial** — passes from the mother to all her children, never from the father (e.g. LHON).
- **Imprinting** — only one parent's copy is active. The same chromosome-15 deletion causes Prader–Willi syndrome if it comes from the father, and Angelman syndrome if from the mother.
- **Linkage** — genes close together travel together.
- **Polygenic / multifactorial** — no simple ratios.
- **Anticipation** — repeated DNA stretches grow each generation, so the disease starts earlier (Huntington's, fragile X).

**What blurs even single-gene patterns**
- **Penetrance** — not every carrier shows the trait.
- **Expressivity** — those who show it show it to different degrees.
- **Epistasis** — one gene masks another.
- **Pleiotropy** — one gene has many effects (the sickle-cell gene affects blood, spleen, joints and malaria resistance).

**Uses of Mendel in man:** carrier detection, genetic counselling, cousin-marriage risk (9.3-B), screening programmes, and blood groups as population markers (9.6).

> **Verdict:** ==Mendel explains the **units** of heredity; most human variation comes from many units acting together, with the environment.==

## 6. The four names to know

| Name | Remember them for |
|---|---|
| **Mendel** (1865) | the laws of segregation and independent assortment |
| **Garrod** (1902) | alkaptonuria — the first human recessive trait |
| **Cuénot** (1905) | yellow mice — the 2:1 lethal ratio |
| **Fisher** (1918) | many Mendelian genes produce continuous variation |

## The traps
1. X-linked recessive is **never father → son**. X-linked dominant goes father → **all daughters**.
2. **Codominance** (both alleles show) ≠ **incomplete dominance** (in between).
3. **Polygenic** (genes only) ≠ **multifactorial** (genes + environment).
4. **Huntington's** is dominant *and* fatal, but persists because it acts late.
