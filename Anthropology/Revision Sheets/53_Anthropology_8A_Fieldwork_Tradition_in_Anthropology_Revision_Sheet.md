## 1. Definitions

🪝 *"Ethnography" is both what you **do** — the months in the village — and what you **write**, the book. *Argonauts* is an ethnography in both senses.*

- **Fieldwork:** first-hand, usually long-term research among a living community. You live with the people, learn their language and take part in daily life.
- **Ethnography:** ==(1) the **process** — fieldwork centred on participant observation; (2) the **product** — the written account of a people's way of life.== From Greek *ethnos* (people) + *graphein* (to write).
- **Ethnology:** comparing cultures and building theory from ethnographic material. **Ethnography describes; ethnology compares.**

## 2. The five phases — the history questions (1998, 2011, 2014, 2016, 2018)

### Phase 1 — Armchair (to c. 1890)
🪝 *Asked whether he had ever met the "savages" he wrote about, Frazer is said to have replied "God forbid!"* (This is an anecdote — call it one.)

- Scholars worked from second-hand reports by travellers, missionaries and officials.
- **Key names:** Tylor, *Primitive Culture* (1871); **Frazer, *The Golden Bough* (1890)**.
- ==***Notes and Queries on Anthropology* (1874)**== — a question handbook for amateurs collecting data in the field.
- **The exception:** Morgan worked directly with the Seneca, helped by Ely Parker — *League of the Iroquois* (1851).
- **Weakness:** the data were untested, uneven and prejudiced.

### Phase 2 — Expedition (1883–1910s)
🪝 *Boas's doctorate was on the **colour of seawater**. In 1883 he went to Baffin Island to study geography, wintered with the Inuit, and came back an anthropologist.*

- **Boas:** Baffin Island (1883–84); the **Kwakiutl** from 1886, with **George Hunt** recording texts. Native-language texts, salvage ethnography.
- ==**Torres Strait Expedition (1898)**== — A. C. Haddon with W. H. R. Rivers, C. G. Seligman, C. S. Myers, William McDougall and Sidney Ray.
- **Rivers:** the **genealogical method** (1900; fully set out in 1910), and a call for **"intensive work"** — one worker staying a year or more (1913).
- **Radcliffe-Brown:** the Andaman Islands (1906–08).
- **Weakness:** short stays and interpreters; the observer stayed outside daily life.

### Phase 3 — Malinowski and the classics (1915–1960s)
🪝 *1914: Malinowski, an Austro-Hungarian subject caught in wartime Australia, was allowed to keep researching. He spent 1915–18 in a tent in the **Trobriands**.*

**Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922)** sets out three principles:
1. **Scientific aims** — go with theoretical problems in mind.
2. **Good conditions** — live among the people, away from other Europeans.
3. **Special methods** — collect and fix the evidence systematically.

It also names three kinds of data:
- ==**Skeleton** — the "tribal constitution": genealogies, maps, censuses, **synoptic charts**.==
- ==**Flesh** — the "**imponderabilia of actual life**", kept in a field diary.==
- ==**Spirit** — the ***corpus inscriptionum***: narratives, sayings, spells.==

**The aim:** ==to grasp **the native's point of view**, his relation to life, his vision of his world== (close paraphrase).

**The classical monographs**
- Radcliffe-Brown, *The Andaman Islanders* (1922)
- **Mead, *Coming of Age in Samoa* (1928)**
- Firth, *We, the Tikopia* (1936)
- **Evans-Pritchard** on the Azande (1937) and ***The Nuer* (1940)**
- Fortes on the Tallensi

These books study one people in one place, written in the "ethnographic present", holistically and led by theory.

🪝 *1967: Malinowski's private **diary** was published — lonely, irritable and contemptuous. The serene observer was partly a literary pose.*

### Phase 4 — Critique (1967–1990)
🪝 *Mead was 23 when she found a carefree Samoan adolescence. In 1983, after her death, **Freeman** said two young women had been **joking** with her. The case is still disputed.*

- **The Diary**, 1967
- ==**Asad (ed.), *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973)**== — fieldwork rested on colonial power
- ==**Laura Nader (1972): "studying up"**== — study the powerful too
- **Freeman** on Mead's Samoa, 1983
- ***Writing Culture* (1986)** — ethnographic truths are partial (6-J)
- **Native, feminist and "halfie" anthropologists** — Abu-Lughod (1991)

### Phase 5 — Remade (1990s onward) — the 2020 and 2025 notes
🪝 *Anna Tsing followed the **matsutake** mushroom from Oregon's forests to Japan's markets — people, fungi and pines are all actors in her book (2015).*

| Form | What it is | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Multi-sited** | Follow the people, thing, metaphor, story, life or conflict across linked sites | ==**Marcus 1995**== |
| **Multispecies** | Non-humans as participants in social life | ==**Kirksey & Helmreich 2010**==; Tsing 2015; Haraway |
| **Critical** | Fieldwork committed to exposing and challenging injustice | Thomas 1993; Madison 2005 |
| **Experiential** | The ethnographer's body and feelings as data | Rosaldo 1989; **Stoller 1989**; Jackson |
| **Digital** | Online worlds as field sites | **Boellstorff, *Coming of Age in Second Life* (2008)** |
| **Collaborative / auto-ethnography** | Co-research with the community; one's own group as the field | Lassiter 2005; Reed-Danahay 1997 |

> **Answer line:** "Each phase answered the weakness of the one before: the armchair lacked **data**, the expedition **depth**, the lone fieldworker **reflexivity**."

## 3. Characteristics of fieldwork (2017) — list all ten

1. **Long-term residence** — a full annual cycle.
2. **Participant observation.**
3. **The local language.**
4. **Holism.**
5. **Emic checked by etic.**
6. **Rapport and key informants.**
7. **Several tools, triangulated** (8-C).
8. **Inductive and qualitative.**
9. **Small scale and intensive.**
10. **Reflexivity and ethics.**

**How fieldwork produced theory (1999)**
- Functionalism ← the Trobriands (6-C)
- Segmentary lineage ← the Nuer (4-A)
- Historical particularism ← Boas (6-B)
- Dominant caste and Sanskritisation ← Srinivas in Coorg and Rampura
- Culture and personality ← Samoa (6-E)

Fieldwork can also *unmake* theory: Leach (1954) found Kachin political systems oscillating rather than stable.

## 4. India

🪝 *1951–52: S. C. Dube's team lived in **Shamirpet**, a village near Hyderabad, and wrote *Indian Village* (1955) — one of the first whole-village studies by an Indian.*

- **Colonial survey:** Risley (Census 1901); Thurston, *Castes and Tribes of Southern India* (1909).
- ==**S. C. Roy** — "father of Indian ethnography": *The Mundas and Their Country* (1912), *The Oraons* (1915).==
- **Elwin:** *The Baiga* (1939).
- **Haimendorf:** the Chenchus, Reddis, Raj Gonds of Adilabad and the Apatanis.
- **Village studies:** Srinivas (Rampura; *The Remembered Village*, 1976); Dube (1955); Marriott (ed.), *Village India* (1955); D. N. Majumdar.
- **Anthropological Survey of India** (1945); **People of India** (K. S. Singh, 1985–92).

## 5. Contemporary challenges (2024)

| Challenge | Response |
|---|---|
| Access, gatekeeping, permits | Negotiated access; collaboration |
| Consent with vulnerable or non-literate groups; review boards | Oral, ongoing consent; local protocols |
| Positionality — gender, caste, nation, class | Reflexivity; team fieldwork |
| Safety in conflict zones | Risk planning; remote methods |
| The unbounded field — migration, digital life | Multi-sited and digital ethnography |
| Time and funding | Rapid appraisal; ==**patchwork ethnography** (Günel, Varma & Watanabe 2020)== |
| Communities read back and contest the account | Sharing findings; co-authorship |
| Data protection | Anonymisation; secure storage |

## 6. Ethics (2026)

🪝 *1965: the US Army's **Project Camelot**, an insurgency study planned for Chile, was exposed and cancelled. Anthropologists then wrote their first ethics statements.*

- **AAA milestones:** Statement on Problems of Anthropological Research (1967) · ==**Principles of Professional Responsibility (1971)**== · Codes of Ethics (1998, 2009) · Statement on Ethics (2012). Britain's ASA has its own guidelines.
- **Principles:**
  - ==**do no harm**==
  - ==**informed consent**== (oral and ongoing)
  - **openness** about purpose and funding
  - **confidentiality and anonymity**
  - your **first obligation is to the people studied**
  - **return findings and share benefits**
- **Cases:** Camelot (1965) · covert research in Thailand (1970) · the **Yanomami** controversy after *Darkness in El Dorado* (2000) · the **Human Terrain System** (2007).
- **India:** consent with PVTG and forest communities; respect for sacred knowledge; ICMR guidelines (2017); returning findings in local languages.

## 7. Comparisons

| Basis | Armchair | Field |
|---|---|---|
| Data | Second-hand reports | First-hand observation |
| Scope | World-wide comparison | One community in depth |
| Theory | Evolutionary stages | Function, structure, meaning |

| Basis | Boas | Malinowski |
|---|---|---|
| Field | Repeated visits | Continuous residence |
| Data | Native texts, collaborators | Participant observation, diary |
| Aim | History of particular cultures | How institutions work now |

| Basis | Classical | Contemporary |
|---|---|---|
| Field | A bounded village | Networks, sites, screens, species |
| Researcher | Lone Western outsider | Native, team, collaborator |
| Authority | "I was there" | Reflexive, dialogic |
| First duty | To science | To the people studied |

## The traps

1. **Ethnography ≠ ethnology** — one describes, the other compares.
2. **Rivers came before Malinowski** — genealogy and "intensive work".
3. **Mead–Freeman is disputed** — present both sides.
4. **Frazer's "God forbid!" is an anecdote.**
5. For "contemporary" questions, give **a challenge plus a response** for each point.
