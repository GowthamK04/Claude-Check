## 2. The spine and the tools

| Step | Answer-ready opener |
|---|---|
| **Definition** | ‘X is a constitutionally mandated / statutory … under Art. …’ |
| **Why** | ‘The framers created it to …’ |
| **Basis** | ‘Its basis lies in Art. …, read with … (case, year).’ |
| **How** | ‘In practice it operates through three channels: …’ |
| **Gains** | ‘Its record is real: … (evidence).’ |
| **Gaps** | ‘However, three gaps persist: …’ |
| **Reforms** | ‘…, enacted by …, so that … must answer to …’ (mechanism → institution → accountability) |
| **Judgement** | ‘On balance, X has … ; its future depends on …’ |

**Lenses (pick 2–3):** constitutional · institutional · democratic · federal · judicial · reform/comparative.

**Verbs:**
- Discuss: both sides, then a verdict.
- Examine: factor by factor, each one proved.
- Critically examine: merits and demerits, then a weighted verdict.
- Comment: state your position first, then the reasons.
- Analyse: group by cause and effect.
- Explain: concept → mechanism → example.
- To what extent: give the degree first (‘largely, but…’).
- Suggest: give three-part reform lines.
- Compare: use the same axes for both, ideally as a table.

**Budgets:**
- 10 marks: 150 words in 7 minutes (intro 20, 3–4 points × 25, 1 reform line, close 20).
- 15 marks: 250 words in 11 minutes (intro 30, 2 sub-heads × 3 points, 2–3 reform lines, close 20–30).

**Openers:** a definition · an Article · a data point · a case · a 2025–26 event.

**Closers:** a constitutional value · a committee’s fix · a forward-looking line.

**Diagrams:** a flowchart for a procedure · a 3-column comparison table · a hub-and-spoke for federal institutions · a timeline for a case ladder.

## 3. Answer errors that cost marks

- Time limits for assent come from **Punchhi**, not Sarkaria.
- ‘Deemed assent’ was **rejected on 20 Nov 2025**.
- The right to vote is **statutory** (Jyoti Basu 1982, Kuldip Nayar 2006); casting a vote is expression under 19(1)(a) (PUCL 2003).
- The collegium is **case law** (1993, 1998); NJAC was struck down in 2015.
- **ONOE** is still the 129th Amendment Bill before the JPC; the Kovind report came in 2024.
- The **106th** is not yet operational: it waits for the census and delimitation. The 131st Bill failed in April 2026.
- **Tribunals:** the 2021 Act’s tenure and age-bar provisions were struck on 19 Nov 2025, and a National Tribunals Commission was ordered.
- The Speaker’s **three months** is a judicial benchmark, not constitutional text.

## 4. Thirteen theme skeletons

### T1 · Local government and devolution (11 Qs, last 2026)
**Plain:** The Constitution created a third tier of government in 1992, but states decide how much power, money and staff it actually gets.

- **Definition:** Panchayats and municipalities are constitutionally mandated institutions of self-government (Parts IX and IX-A, 73rd and 74th Amendments, 1992).
- **Why:** They bring decisions close to the people affected (subsidiarity), widen representation (one-third women, SC/ST seats) and improve service delivery.
- **Basis:** Art. 40 (DPSP); Arts. 243–243O and 243P–243ZG; Eleventh (29) and Twelfth (18) Schedules; SEC (243K/243ZA); SFC (243I/243Y); DPC and MPC (243ZD–ZE); PESA 1996.
- **How:** Three tiers, five-year terms, elections by the SEC within six months of dissolution, devolution of functions by state law, grants on SFC advice.
- **Gains:** Several lakh elected representatives with a large share of women (MoPR); political entry for SC/ST and women; gram sabha as direct democracy; 15th FC grants tied to audited accounts.
- **Gaps:** The 3Fs gap: functions listed but not transferred, thin own revenue, staff controlled by state departments. Proxy representation by male relatives. Parastatals bypass city councils. Weak mayors. SFCs delayed. Elections postponed until the SC intervened.
- **Reforms:**
  - **Statutory activity mapping that assigns each Eleventh/Twelfth Schedule subject to a tier with matching funds and staff**. Institution: State legislature, with MoPR’s devolution index as the benchmark. Accountability: The SFC and the CAG report devolution gaps to the assembly each year.
  - **Grants conditional on audited accounts and a property-tax floor**. Institution: Finance Commission and state governments. Accountability: Unaudited bodies lose grants, so councils must publish accounts.
  - **Directly elected mayors with a fixed five-year term and control over city agencies**. Institution: State municipal law. Accountability: Mayors answer to voters and councils, not to state-appointed commissioners.
- **Judgement:** *The architecture of 1992 is in place; what is missing is ‘functionality’ — real power, money and staff at the third tier.*
- **Pegs:** 2nd ARC 6th Report (Local Governance, 2007) · Balwant Rai Mehta 1957, Ashok Mehta 1977, L.M. Singhvi 1986 · Vikas Kishanrao Gawali 2021 (triple test for OBC seats) · Suresh Mahajan 2022 (no postponing local polls) · RBI Report on Municipal Finances 2024
- **Asked as:** 2026 women’s reservation from local bodies to Parliament · 2025 decisions far from information · 2024 merging rural and urban bodies · 2023 states reluctant to empower ULBs · 2022 decentralisation · 2020 ‘functionality’

### T2 · Federalism, Centre–state relations and asymmetry (16 Qs, last 2026)
**Plain:** India is a union that holds diverse units together under a strong Centre, with special arrangements for some regions.

- **Definition:** A ‘Union of States’ (Art. 1): an indestructible Union of destructible states, federal in form with a unitary bias, often called quasi-federal (K.C. Wheare) or cooperative (Granville Austin).
- **Why:** To keep a diverse country united while letting regions govern themselves; asymmetry accommodates distinct identities (Fifth/Sixth Schedules, Art. 371).
- **Basis:** Seventh Schedule; Arts. 245–263 (legislative, administrative), 268–293 and 280 (financial), 279A (GST Council), 356, 365; federalism is a basic feature (Bommai 1994).
- **How:** Distribution of Lists; override clauses (249–254); Finance Commission transfers; GST Council pooling; Inter-State and Zonal Councils; NITI Governing Council.
- **Gains:** Linguistic reorganisation stabilised the Union; GST pooled fiscal sovereignty; 14th and 15th FC raised the states’ share of the divisible pool to 42% and 41%; Art. 356 use fell sharply after Bommai.
- **Gaps:** Centralising laws in crises (Epidemic Diseases Act, DM Act — Mains 2020); cesses and surcharges outside the divisible pool; Governors in conflict with elected governments; central agencies and consent withdrawals; delimitation and the North–South representation question; GST compensation ended in 2022.
- **Reforms:**
  - **Bring cesses and surcharges beyond a cap into the divisible pool**. Institution: Parliament, by amending Arts. 270–271, with the Finance Commission monitoring. Accountability: The FC reports the cap’s breach to Parliament, which must justify it.
  - **A standing Inter-State Council with its own secretariat and fixed meeting cycle**. Institution: President’s order under Art. 263, as Punchhi recommended. Accountability: An annual report on Centre–state disputes laid before Parliament.
  - **Appoint Governors after consulting the Chief Minister, with fixed tenure**. Institution: Constitutional amendment of Arts. 155–156 (Sarkaria, Punchhi). Accountability: Removal only for stated cause, reviewable by courts.
- **Judgement:** *Indian federalism has proved flexible, not fragile; its future depends on institutional trust between the Centre and the states, not just on transfers.*
- **Pegs:** Rajamannar 1969 · Sarkaria 1988 · Punchhi 2010 · State of WB v Union 1962 · Bommai 1994 · Mohit Minerals 2022 · GNCTD 2018, 2023 · In re Art. 370 (2023) · 131st Amendment Bill defeat (Apr 2026)
- **Asked as:** 2026 accommodation of diversity and dispute resolution · 2025 fiscal federalism · 2024 recent changes and trust-building · 2023 decline of Art. 356 · 2020 cooperation, competition, confrontation

### T3 · Governor and the state legislature (4 Qs, last 2026)
**Plain:** The Governor is the state’s constitutional head who normally acts on the elected government’s advice, but holds a few discretionary powers that have become flashpoints.

- **Definition:** A centrally appointed constitutional head of the state (Art. 153) who acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers except where the Constitution allows discretion (Art. 163).
- **Why:** A link between the Union and the state, and a guardian of constitutional processes when the elected machinery fails.
- **Basis:** Arts. 153–167, 163, 174 (summoning), 200–201 (assent), 213 (ordinances), 356 (report); Shamsher Singh 1974, Nabam Rebia 2016, B.P. Singhal 2010.
- **How:** Assent options under Art. 200: assent, withhold, return, reserve; discretion in appointing a CM in a hung House and in reporting under Art. 356.
- **Gains:** Continuity during political crises; reservation protects the High Court and Union interests; the 2025 opinion said Governors cannot sit on Bills indefinitely.
- **Gaps:** Bills held for months or years; partisan use of discretion; removal at pleasure; no time limit in Art. 200; the April 2025 timelines and ‘deemed assent’ were overturned by the 20 November 2025 opinion.
- **Reforms:**
  - **Amend Art. 200 to set a time limit for decisions on Bills**. Institution: Parliament, by constitutional amendment (Punchhi suggested six months for reserved Bills). Accountability: Delay beyond the limit becomes a reviewable breach of a constitutional duty.
  - **A binding code of conduct for Governors on discretion and appointments**. Institution: Inter-State Council adopts it; President issues it. Accountability: Departures must be recorded with reasons, which the Assembly can debate.
  - **Speaker to decide disqualification petitions within three months**. Institution: Tenth Schedule amendment, codifying Padi Kaushik Reddy (2025). Accountability: Missed deadlines open the decision to judicial review.
- **Judgement:** *The Governor is meant to be a constitutional sentinel, not a parallel government; timelines and reasons would restore that design.*
- **Pegs:** Shamsher Singh 1974 · Rameshwar Prasad 2006 · B.P. Singhal 2010 · Nabam Rebia 2016 · Krishna Kumar Singh 2017 · State of TN v Governor (Apr 2025) · Presidential Reference opinion (20 Nov 2025) · Sarkaria, Punchhi
- **Asked as:** 2026 Governor’s assent and aid and advice · 2023 presiding officers · 2022 Governor’s ordinances · 2021 Legislative Councils

### T4 · Judiciary: independence and accountability (5 Qs, last 2025)
**Plain:** Judges must be free from pressure to decide fairly, but they must also answer for misconduct; India has protected the first far better than the second.

- **Definition:** Independence is insulation of judges’ appointment, tenure, pay and removal from the other branches; accountability is answerability for conduct and performance.
- **Why:** Courts enforce limits on power, so they must be free; public trust requires that they also be answerable.
- **Basis:** Arts. 50, 124, 217, 121 and 211 (no discussion of judges’ conduct), 129/215 (contempt); Judges (Inquiry) Act 1968; Three Judges Cases; NJAC case 2015; in-house procedure (1999).
- **How:** Collegium recommendations; fixed retirement ages; salaries charged on the Consolidated Fund; removal only by special-majority address of both Houses.
- **Gains:** Independence secured against the executive; collegium resolutions published since 2017; the 2025–26 Varma inquiry showed the Judges (Inquiry) Act can work to the point of findings.
- **Gaps:** Opaque collegium; Memorandum of Procedure not finalised; vacancies and delays; low diversity; post-retirement posts; no judge ever removed — Justice Varma resigned in April 2026 before the committee found the charges proved.
- **Reforms:**
  - **Finalise the Memorandum of Procedure with timelines and a secretariat**. Institution: Supreme Court collegium with the Union Law Ministry. Accountability: Reasons and timelines are published, so delays can be traced.
  - **Amend the Judges (Inquiry) Act so inquiries continue after resignation and findings carry consequences**. Institution: Parliament. Accountability: Resignation can no longer erase the record of misconduct.
  - **A cooling-off period before post-retirement appointments**. Institution: Parliament by law, or a full-court resolution. Accountability: Removes the incentive to please the executive before retirement.
- **Judgement:** *Independence and accountability are not rivals; constitutional morality requires both, and the gap today is on accountability.*
- **Pegs:** S.P. Gupta 1981 · SCAORA 1993 · In re 1998 · C. Ravichandran Iyer 1995 · SCAORA (NJAC) 2015 · Manoj Narula 2014 (constitutional morality) · NCRWC 2002 (National Judicial Commission) · Justice Varma inquiry (2025–26)
- **Asked as:** 2025 collegium vs USA · 2025 constitutional morality and accountability · 2023 judicial independence and democracy · 2021 women in higher judiciary · 2017 NJAC judgment

### T5 · Judicial review, PIL and separation of powers (5 Qs, last 2026)
**Plain:** Courts test laws and actions against the Constitution; through PIL they have also issued guidelines when the legislature was silent.

- **Definition:** Judicial review is the power to invalidate unconstitutional laws and actions; judicial legislation is court-made guidelines filling a legislative vacuum.
- **Why:** It enforces constitutional limits and protects rights of those who cannot approach courts themselves.
- **Basis:** Arts. 13, 32, 136, 141, 142, 226; India follows checks and balances, not strict separation (Ram Jawaya Kapur 1955); separation of powers is a basic feature (Raj Narain 1975).
- **How:** Relaxed locus standi and epistolary jurisdiction; guidelines (Vishaka 1997, D.K. Basu 1997, Prakash Singh 2006, Common Cause 2018); continuing mandamus.
- **Gains:** Rights expansion; environmental constitutionalism (M.C. Mehta cases — Mains 2022); prison, police and custodial reforms.
- **Gaps:** Overreach and weak compliance; PIL misuse; docket pressure; Art. 142 stretched (the April 2025 ‘deemed assent’ was rejected by the November 2025 reference).
- **Reforms:**
  - **Rules for screening PILs, with costs for frivolous petitions**. Institution: Supreme Court rules under Art. 145. Accountability: Admission becomes reasoned and reviewable.
  - **Dialogic remedies: courts set principles and timelines, legislatures enact**. Institution: Parliament and state legislatures, as after Vishaka (POSH Act 2013). Accountability: Compliance hearings track whether the legislature acted.
  - **A permanent constitutional division of the Supreme Court**. Institution: Parliament and the SC (Law Commission 229th Report, 2009). Accountability: Constitutional questions decided on time, by benches of known strength.
- **Judgement:** *Judicial activism is legitimate when it fills a rights gap and recedes when institutions act; restraint and activism are both duties.*
- **Pegs:** Ram Jawaya Kapur 1955 · Kesavananda 1973 · S.P. Gupta 1981 · Vishaka 1997 · Vineet Narain 1997 · Prakash Singh 2006 · Common Cause 2018 · Presidential Reference 2025
- **Asked as:** 2026 judicial legislation and euthanasia · 2024 growth of PIL · 2020 judicial legislation vs separation of powers · 2019 checks and balances · 2014 judicial activism

### T6 · Parliament, committees and executive accountability (16 Qs, last 2024)
**Plain:** Parliament’s job is to make the executive answer every day, not just at elections; its tools exist, but they are used less.

- **Definition:** Parliamentary accountability is the continuous answerability of the Council of Ministers to the Lok Sabha (Art. 75(3)).
- **Why:** It turns a five-yearly mandate into daily control over law, money and policy.
- **Basis:** Arts. 75, 79–122, 105 (privileges), 108 (joint sitting), 110 (Money Bill); Rules of Procedure; financial committees and DRSCs.
- **How:** Question Hour, Zero Hour, motions; PAC, Estimates and COPU; 24 DRSCs; budget scrutiny; Rajya Sabha as revising chamber.
- **Gains:** PAC reports on major audits; DRSC scrutiny of bills; the Rajya Sabha’s revising role (Mains 2020).
- **Gaps:** Fewer sittings (the 17th Lok Sabha held 274); falling referral of bills to committees (PRS: 60% in the 14th, 71% in the 15th, 27% in the 16th, 16% in the 17th Lok Sabha); Money Bill route to bypass the Rajya Sabha; the Deputy Speaker’s post left vacant through the 17th Lok Sabha; whip and anti-defection curb MPs (Mains 2013, 2019).
- **Reforms:**
  - **A minimum number of sitting days (NCRWC suggested 120 for the Lok Sabha, 100 for the Rajya Sabha)**. Institution: Parliament, by law or its Rules. Accountability: Shortfalls are reported to the House and the public each year.
  - **Default referral of bills to committees unless the Speaker records reasons**. Institution: Rules of Procedure of each House. Accountability: Bypassing scrutiny requires a recorded, public justification.
  - **Limit whips to confidence and Money Bills**. Institution: Tenth Schedule amendment (Law Commission 170th Report, 1999; Dinesh Goswami 1990). Accountability: MPs vote on other bills by conscience and answer to their voters.
- **Judgement:** *Parliament has not lost its powers; it has lost the habit of using them.*
- **Pegs:** PRS Legislative Research data · NCRWC 2002 · Law Commission 170th Report 1999 · Kihoto 1992 · Rojer Mathew 2019 · Sita Soren 2024 (privilege)
- **Asked as:** 2024 cabinet system and parliamentary supremacy · 2023 committee system · 2022 VP as RS Chairman · 2021 executive accountability, DRSCs · 2020 Rajya Sabha · 2019 decline of the MP

### T7 · Elections, the ECI, the Speaker and anti-defection (12 Qs, last 2026)
**Plain:** Free elections need an umpire the government cannot capture, rules that keep money and crime out, and a fair referee for defections.

- **Definition:** The electoral system is the constitutional and statutory machinery (Arts. 324–329, RPA 1950 and 1951, Tenth Schedule) that converts votes into mandates and keeps them stable.
- **Why:** Free and fair elections are part of the basic structure (Raj Narain 1975); without them every other institution loses legitimacy.
- **Basis:** Art. 324 (ECI), 326 (adult suffrage), 329 (bar on courts mid-election); RPA 1950 (rolls), 1951 (conduct, disqualification); CEC Act 2023; Tenth Schedule; MCC (not statutory).
- **How:** ECI superintendence and the MCC; roll revision under RPA 1950; election petitions in High Courts; Speaker decides defections.
- **Gains:** Peaceful transfers of power; EVMs with VVPAT; NOTA; electoral bonds struck down (2024) for transparency.
- **Gaps:** Money power; criminalisation (ADR found 46% of Lok Sabha MPs elected in 2024 declared criminal cases); executive-heavy ECI panel; partisan Speakers and delays (Telangana 2025); defections via resignations; disputes over roll revision.
- **Reforms:**
  - **Disqualification petitions decided by an independent tribunal, or by the President or Governor on the ECI’s binding advice**. Institution: Constitutional amendment of the Tenth Schedule (Dinesh Goswami 1990; Meghachandra 2020; Padi Kaushik Reddy 2025). Accountability: Decisions become time-bound, reasoned and free of party interest.
  - **A CEC selection panel that includes the CJI**. Institution: Parliament, amending the 2023 Act (Tarkunde 1975; Law Commission 255th Report 2015). Accountability: Appointments gain bipartisan legitimacy.
  - **Disqualification on framing of charges for serious offences, with safeguards**. Institution: Parliament, amending the RPA (Law Commission 244th Report 2014). Accountability: Parties face a legal cost for fielding such candidates.
- **Judgement:** *India runs elections with rare competence; the reform frontier is the independence of the referees — the ECI and the Speaker.*
- **Pegs:** Raj Narain 1975 · Jyoti Basu 1982 / Kuldip Nayar 2006 (right to vote statutory) · PUCL 2003 (voting as expression) · Lal Babu Hussein 1995 · Kihoto 1992 · Lily Thomas 2013 · Anoop Baranwal 2023 · ADR 2024 · Kovind Committee 2024 (ONOE; 129th Bill with the JPC)
- **Asked as:** 2026 right to vote and roll revision · 2026 Speaker’s neutrality · 2025 corrupt practices · 2024 ONOE · 2022 MCC · 2022 election disputes · 2020 ‘once a Speaker, always a Speaker’

### T8 · Tribunals, quasi-judicial bodies and regulators (10 Qs, last 2025)
**Plain:** Specialised bodies decide disputes faster than courts, but only if the government does not control who sits on them.

- **Definition:** Tribunals are specialised adjudicatory bodies under Arts. 323A/323B or statutes; quasi-judicial bodies decide disputes like courts without being courts.
- **Why:** Expertise, speed and lower cost, and relief for the regular courts.
- **Basis:** Arts. 323A, 323B (42nd Amendment), 227; L. Chandra Kumar 1997 (High Court review); Madras Bar Association cases 2010, 2014, 2020, 2021, 2025.
- **How:** Statutory tribunals (CAT, NGT, NCLT, ITAT); regulators (CCI, SEBI, TRAI); Lok Adalats and arbitration as alternative dispute resolution (Mains 2024).
- **Gains:** Specialised adjudication in tax, company, environment and service law; NGT’s environmental jurisprudence.
- **Gaps:** Executive control over selection, tenure and pay; vacancies; appeals straight to the SC; on 19 November 2025 the SC struck down the 2021 Act’s four-year tenure and 50-year age bar as a re-enactment of what it had already invalidated.
- **Reforms:**
  - **A National Tribunals Commission to run appointments, service conditions and administration**. Institution: Parliament (SC direction of Nov 2025; a 2026 Bill passed the Lok Sabha in August 2026). Accountability: Tribunals report to an independent body, reviewable by courts, not to the parent ministry.
  - **Tenure and age rules fixed in line with the Madras Bar judgments**. Institution: Parliament. Accountability: Members cannot be pressured through reappointment.
  - **Appeals to High Court division benches rather than straight to the SC**. Institution: Parliament, amending parent statutes. Accountability: Restores the High Courts’ supervisory role (L. Chandra Kumar).
- **Judgement:** *Tribunals are justified only if they are as independent as the courts they replace.*
- **Pegs:** R.K. Jain 1993 · L. Chandra Kumar 1997 · Madras Bar Association 2010, 2014, 2020, 2021, 19 Nov 2025 · Rojer Mathew 2019
- **Asked as:** 2025 tribunal rationalisation · 2024 Lok Adalats vs arbitration · 2023 CCI · 2019 CAT · 2018 tribunals vs courts · 2016 quasi-judicial bodies · 2015 regulators’ independence

### T9 · Constitutional and statutory bodies (20 Qs, last 2026)
**Plain:** Watchdogs like the CAG, the Finance Commission and the rights commissions are only as strong as their independence and their power to make governments act.

- **Definition:** Constitutional bodies (CAG, FC, UPSC, NCSC, NCST, NCBC) draw powers from the Constitution; statutory bodies (NHRC, CIC, CVC, Lokpal) from Acts of Parliament.
- **Why:** They supply expert, continuous accountability that elected bodies cannot.
- **Basis:** Arts. 148–151, 280, 315–323, 338–338B; PHRA 1993, RTI 2005, CVC 2003, Lokpal 2013; 102nd and 105th Amendments (NCBC; states’ SEBC lists).
- **How:** Audit (CAG), fiscal transfers (FC), recruitment (UPSC), rights monitoring and inquiry (NHRC, NCSC/NCST), disclosure (CIC), vigilance (CVC, Lokpal).
- **Gains:** CAG’s performance audits beyond legality (Mains 2024: propriety); FC-driven devolution; RTI as a citizen’s right; NCBC’s constitutional status (2018).
- **Gaps:** Recommendatory powers; selection panels dominated by the executive; the 2019 RTI amendment and the 2023 DPDP change; vacancies; CAG reports wait for PAC discussion.
- **Reforms:**
  - **Findings of NHRC, NCSC and NCST binding unless rejected with written reasons**. Institution: Parliament, amending the PHRA and adding to Arts. 338–338A. Accountability: Governments must justify non-compliance before the legislature.
  - **Commissioners’ tenure and pay fixed in the statute**. Institution: Parliament, amending the RTI Act. Accountability: The executive can no longer vary terms to signal displeasure.
  - **A permanent Finance Commission secretariat**. Institution: Parliament, amending the FC Act 1951. Accountability: Continuity and data between commissions, reported to Parliament.
- **Judgement:** *Constitutional status gives these bodies dignity; independence and enforceable follow-up give them effect.*
- **Pegs:** Vineet Narain 1997 · Jaishri Patil 2021 and the 105th Amendment · RTI Amendment 2019 · DPDP 2023 · Lokpal 2019 · steps to constitutionalise a body: an Art. 368 amendment inserting it (as the 102nd did for the NCBC)
- **Asked as:** 2026 NCSC/NCST enforcement · 2025 Attorney General · 2024 CAG propriety · 2022 NCBC · 2021 NHRC limits · 2020 RTI amendment · 2020 constitutionalising the NCW

### T10 · Rights and the expanding Article 21 (12 Qs, last 2026)
**Plain:** The Supreme Court has read ‘life and personal liberty’ to cover dignity, privacy, environment and identity, making the Constitution a living document.

- **Definition:** The ‘living instrument’ approach reads Fundamental Rights expansively and as linked, not as separate codes (Cooper 1970, Maneka 1978).
- **Why:** Rights must grow with society; a static text would fail new problems such as data, climate and gender identity.
- **Basis:** Arts. 14, 15(3), 19, 21, 21A, 32; DPSP as interpretive guides; Puttaswamy 2017 (privacy); NALSA 2014 (gender identity).
- **How:** Interpretation by courts; follow-up statutes (POSH 2013, Transgender Persons Act 2019, DPDP 2023).
- **Gains:** Dignity, livelihood, environment, privacy, decriminalisation (Navtej), living wills, climate (Ranjitsinh 2024); gender justice through Vishaka, Shayara Bano 2017, Joseph Shine 2018, Babita Puniya 2020.
- **Gaps:** Rights declared but weakly enforced; the 2026 amendment to the Transgender Act requires medical scrutiny, in tension with NALSA’s self-identification and Puttaswamy’s autonomy (Mains 2026); hate speech vs free speech balance.
- **Reforms:**
  - **Rights-impact assessment for new bills**. Institution: Parliament, through its Rules and committees. Accountability: Bills that dilute recognised rights must say so openly.
  - **Independent oversight of the Data Protection Board**. Institution: Parliament, amending the DPDP Act. Accountability: Privacy is protected from the state as well as from companies.
- **Judgement:** *Article 21 has become the Constitution’s engine of growth; its credibility now depends on enforcement, not new declarations.*
- **Pegs:** Gopalan 1950 · Cooper 1970 · Maneka 1978 · Olga Tellis 1985 · Vishaka 1997 · NALSA 2014 · Shreya Singhal 2015 · Puttaswamy 2017 · Navtej 2018 · Common Cause 2018 · Babita Puniya 2020 · Ranjitsinh 2024
- **Asked as:** 2026 transgender amendment and privacy · 2024 privacy and DNA tests · 2023 living instrument · 2023 gender justice · 2022 movement and residence · 2021 constitutional morality

### T11 · Amending power and constitutional design (5 Qs, last 2025)
**Plain:** Parliament can change the Constitution, but only by a hard procedure and never so as to destroy its basic structure.

- **Definition:** The amending power (Art. 368) is a constituent power exercised by Parliament under procedural and substantive limits.
- **Why:** To let the Constitution adapt without letting a passing majority remake it.
- **Basis:** Art. 368; 24th, 42nd, 44th Amendments; Kesavananda 1973, Raj Narain 1975, Minerva 1980, Waman Rao 1981, Coelho 2007, NJAC 2015.
- **How:** Procedural limits: special majority in each House separately, state ratification for federal provisions, no joint sitting. Substantive limits: basic structure and judicial review of amendments.
- **Gains:** 106 amendments without a rupture; basic structure checked the Emergency-era 39th and 42nd; the 131st Bill’s defeat (April 2026) shows the procedural check working.
- **Gaps:** The basic structure is judge-defined and open-ended; amendments passed with little debate; state ratification rarely contested.
- **Reforms:**
  - **Pre-legislative consultation and committee scrutiny for every amendment bill**. Institution: Parliament, through its Rules (and the 2014 pre-legislative consultation policy). Accountability: Major changes are debated before votes are counted.
- **Judgement:** *The amending power is wide but not unlimited; the basic structure is the Constitution’s identity, and the special majority its everyday guard.*
- **Pegs:** Shankari Prasad 1951 · Golaknath 1967 · Kesavananda 1973 · Minerva 1980 · Coelho 2007 · NJAC 2015 · Balram Singh 2024
- **Asked as:** 2025 procedural and substantive limits · 2019 can Parliament destroy the basic structure · 2016 Coelho · 2016 Preamble’s adjectives · 2016 GoI Act 1935 as federal

### T12 · Comparative constitutions (9 Qs, last 2026)
**Plain:** UPSC asks how India’s choices differ from the US, UK and France — and why.

- **Definition:** Comparative analysis explains Indian design by contrast: what India borrowed, adapted or rejected.
- **Why:** It shows that Indian solutions are deliberate, not accidental.
- **Basis:** Borrowings (UK, USA, Ireland, Canada, Australia, Weimar, USSR, France, South Africa, Japan).
- **How:** Compare on a fixed set of axes: executive, separation of powers, judicial review, amendment, federalism, secularism, equality, pardon, judicial appointments.
- **Gains:** Answers that compare on axes score better than lists of differences.
- **Gaps:** Answers that list differences without fixed axes, and without saying why India chose differently, lose marks.
- **Judgement:** *India blends Westminster government with American-style judicial review, adapted to a diverse, unequal society.*
- **Pegs:** See the comparison grid in Section 4.
- **Asked as:** 2026 separation of powers India vs USA · 2025 pardon India vs USA · 2024 secularism India vs USA · 2023 parliamentary sovereignty UK vs India · 2022 electing Presidents of India and France · 2021 equality USA vs India · 2020 judiciary India vs UK · 2019 France and Indian secularism

### T13 · Parties, pressure groups and civil society (9 Qs, last 2026)
**Plain:** Democracy works through organised interests as well as elections; the question is whether they widen voice or concentrate influence.

- **Definition:** Pressure groups seek to influence policy without contesting power; parties seek to form government; civil society is the organised space between state and market.
- **Why:** They carry excluded interests into policy and hold governments to account between elections.
- **Basis:** Art. 19(1)(a), (b), (c); RPA s. 29A; Symbols Order 1968; FCRA 2010 (2020 amendment).
- **How:** Lobbying, protest, litigation, expert input; Almond and Powell’s four types.
- **Gains:** Farmers’ mobilisation led to repeal of the farm laws (Nov 2021); environmental movements (Chipko, Narmada) shaped law; business associations shape economic policy.
- **Gaps:** No lobbying disclosure law; corporate money and politics (electoral bonds struck down 2024); weak internal party democracy; CIC’s 2013 order making national parties public authorities under RTI never complied with; FCRA tightening (2020).
- **Reforms:**
  - **A lobbying disclosure register**. Institution: Parliament, by law. Accountability: Who met whom on what becomes public.
  - **Internal party democracy and accounts audited and published**. Institution: ECI, under amended RPA s. 29A. Accountability: Parties lose recognition benefits if they do not comply.
- **Judgement:** *Organised interests deepen democracy when they are transparent; they threaten it when influence is hidden.*
- **Pegs:** Almond & Powell · Rajni Kothari (Congress system) · CIC order 2013 · ADR v Union of India 2024 · FCRA Amendment 2020
- **Asked as:** 2026 pressure groups and corporate power · 2025 environmental pressure groups · 2022 national vs regional parties · 2021 business associations · 2019 farmers’ organisations

## 8. Three model answers: the moves to remember

- **A1 Governor (2026, 15 marks).** Position: nominal head, Union link, pleasure that is not arbitrary. Art. 200 options, and the Nov 2025 opinion. Discretion: express (Sixth Schedule para 9, Art. 371, Art. 356 reports) and situational (hung House, sanction to prosecute — MP SPE 2004), but narrow (Nabam Rebia). Reform: Punchhi’s timeline and a code. Close: ‘a sentinel, not a parallel government’.
- **A2 Art. 356 decline (2023, 15 marks).** Legal: Bommai (review, floor test, Assembly only suspended), Rameshwar Prasad 2006, Uttarakhand and Arunachal 2016, the 44th’s one-year limit, Sarkaria’s guidelines. Political: coalitions, Rajya Sabha numbers, Narayanan’s returns (UP 1997, Bihar 1998), media. Still used: J&K, Manipur. Close: ‘from political weapon to emergency brake’.
- **A3 ULBs (2023, 10 marks).** Art. 243W says ‘may’. Functional gaps: parastatals, inactive MPCs, weak mayors. Financial gaps: property tax, GST subsuming octroi, SFCs ignored. Reform: activity mapping, FC-linked property tax, empowered MPCs, elected mayors.
